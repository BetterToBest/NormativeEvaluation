## Theme 1 — The object being scored: the design principles

Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for
Collective Action* (Cambridge, 1990); Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences,
2009. The eight design principles, as stated by the Ostrom Workshop at Indiana
University and in the secondary literature:

1. Clearly defined boundaries (who may appropriate; what the resource is)
2. Congruence between appropriation/provision rules and local conditions
   (Cox et al. split this into 2A congruence with local conditions and
   2B proportional equivalence between benefits and costs)
3. Collective-choice arrangements — those affected by operational rules can
   participate in modifying them
4. Monitoring — monitors are accountable to appropriators, or are appropriators
5. Graduated sanctions
6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms that are cheap and rapidly accessible
7. Minimal recognition of rights to organise by external authorities
8. Nested enterprises — larger CPRs organised in multiple nested layers

The Ostrom Workshop describes the principles as characteristics of robust CPR
institutions, identified as usually *absent* in failed cases as well as present
in successful ones. Ostrom's own later statements: "Design Principles of Robust
Property-Rights Institutions: What Have We Learned" (2009) and "Beyond Markets
and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems," *American
Economic Review* 100(3): 641–672 (2010).

**Key framing for NEEC scoring:** the principles are a *diagnostic* for
institutions that govern a common-pool resource. They are not a theory of
production, distribution, money, or employment. This is the core scoping fact
and it drives most of the Domain 1 and Domain 2 scores.

## Theme 2 — Empirical validation of the principles

**Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás (2010),** "A Review of Design Principles for
Community-based Natural Resource Management," *Ecology and Society* 15(4): 38.
Analysed **91 studies**; found the principles **well supported empirically**;
proposed a reformulation splitting principles 1 and 2 (giving 11 rather than 8).
This is the standard citation for "the principles have been tested."

Recorded critique from the same literature (Salish Sea Wiki summary of Cox et
al.): the principles point at *structural* attributes and do not expose
processes such as trust, legitimacy, and transparency.

**Wilson, Ostrom & Cox (2013),** "Generalizing the core design principles for
the efficacy of groups," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* —
generalises the principles beyond CPRs to group efficacy broadly. Relevant to
C5.5 (cultural adaptability) and to the archetype question.

**Japanese irrigation CPRs** (Sarker & Itoh, *Agricultural Water Management*,
2001): the eight principles account for the success of Japan's long-enduring
irrigation institutions, but principle 7 must be *moderated* — the external
entity (central, prefectural, local government) is non-coercive and has invested
heavily. Monitoring and graduated sanctions are implicit rather than explicit.
Useful evidence that the principles travel but require local adjustment.

## Theme 3 — Scale actually achieved (evidentiary tier: real-world at scale)

**Nepal community forestry — the largest well-documented case.**
- Department of Forests and Soil Conservation (official): **19,361 CFUGs**,
  **1,813,478 ha** handed over, **2,461,549 households** benefiting.
- MoFE (2020), cited in *Forest Policy and Economics* (2026): **over 23,000
  registered CFUGs**, **16.6 million people**, **over 2 million ha (35%)** of
  forested area; CFUGs manage **over 75% of mid-hill forests** (Acharya 2005).
- Forest-department statistics reported 2023: **22,266 CFUGs**, **2,237,670 ha**,
  **~14.45 million people from 2.9 million households**.
- CIFOR/CBFP (Dec 2024): **35% of the population** belongs to CFUGs, managing
  **2.4 million ha**.
- Legal basis: **Forest Act 1993** and Forest Regulation 1995. Programme dates
  from the late 1970s.
- Outcome claim: Nepal's forest cover rose from **39.6% to 44.74%** (2015 report
  on the 2010–2014 forest resource survey).

**NOTE — figures conflict across sources** (19,361 vs 22,266 vs 23,000+ CFUGs;
1.81M vs 2.24M vs 2.4M ha; 14.45M vs 16.6M people). Different vintages and
different counting rules. Disclose the range; do not rest a score on a single
figure.

**Critical counter-evidence from the same case** (MDPI *Sustainability* 2024,
four CFUGs in central Nepal, n=275 households): insufficient funding, technical
expertise and infrastructure; a "blanket approach" and inadequate needs
assessment have limited livelihood initiatives **particularly for marginalized
groups — women, indigenous people, and landless users**. This is direct
evidence for the elite-capture / distributional critique and bears on C4.3,
C4.4 and C1.1.

## Theme 4 — Fisheries co-management: the largest systematic outcome study

**Gutiérrez, Hilborn & Defeo (2011),** "Leadership, social capital and
incentives promote successful fisheries," *Nature* 470(7334): 386–389.
- **130 co-managed fisheries** examined across countries at different
  development levels, ecosystems, sectors and resource types.
- Scored on **8 outcomes** (community empowerment, sustainable catches,
  abundance, prices, etc.).
- **40% scored positively on 6, 7 or 8 outcomes; a further 25% on 4 or 5.**
  Read the other way: **roughly 35% scored positively on 3 or fewer.**
- Most important attribute: **strong leadership**; then individual or
  community quotas, social cohesion, protected areas. Less important:
  enforcement mechanisms, long-term management policies, resource life
  history.
- Context figures from the paper's opening: **one billion people** depend on
  seafood as their primary protein source; **25%** of the world's animal
  protein comes from fisheries; **a third of fish stocks** are overexploited
  or depleted.
- Defeo's own characterisation on publication: Ostrom was right, in that
  fishers can self-organise and sustain the resource.

**NEEC reading.** This is the strongest single piece of evidence for C5.1
(proven components) and C3.x (resilience), and simultaneously the strongest
evidence against a *pass* on reliability: a ~35% weak-outcome rate is not the
90%-style threshold NEEC pass conditions use. The same study is the cleanest
available answer to "does it work at scale, repeatedly?" — the answer is
"often, not reliably."

## Theme 5 — Scale of the commons as a share of the world economy

Rights and Resources Initiative (RRI), *Who Owns the World's Land?* (2nd ed.,
2023), and the ILC/Oxfam/RRI *Common Ground* report (2016):
- **Up to 2.5 billion people** — roughly a third of humanity — depend on land
  and natural resources held, used or managed **collectively**; this includes
  ~370 million Indigenous people.
- Communities **customarily hold or use at least 50%** of the Earth's land,
  but governments legally recognise their ownership of only about **10–11%**
  (RRI's 2023 figure: over 11% across 73 countries covering 85% of global
  land).
- **Over 100 Mha** of additional community land was legally recognised
  2015–2020; at least **39 national governments** increased recognised area.
- Roughly **one third of the world's forests** are under community-based,
  participatory or decentralised management arrangements (World Development,
  2018).

**NEEC reading.** This is a genuinely large real-world footprint — larger than
most systems in the corpus can claim — but it is a footprint in *resource
tenure*, not in the provision of income, housing or employment. The scale
evidence supports Domain 5 strongly and Domain 1 barely at all. It also
supplies the sharpest fact for the legal-recognition question under design
principle 7: the recognition gap (50% held vs 10–11% recognised) is the
mechanism's single largest real-world vulnerability.

## Theme 6 — The critical literature (deliberately sought, per project norms)

- **Agrawal & Gibson (1999), "Enchantment and Disenchantment: The Role of
  Community in Natural Resource Conservation."** Communities are not
  homogeneous; the CPR literature's "community" can obscure internal
  inequality. Agrawal & Gibson (2001) add the sharper point: apparent
  cooperation may be **cooperation imposed on low-power actors** through
  social and economic power.
- **Saunders (2014)** (as summarised in the secondary literature): CPR theory
  tends to be detached from context, explaining institutions through voluntary
  exchange and expected shared benefit rather than through **conflictual and
  unequal power relations**.
- **Elite capture is a standing, named finding**, not a fringe objection —
  cited across Agrawal & Gupta (2005), Blaikie (2006), Ribot et al. (2006),
  Sikor & Nguyen (2007), Nelson & Agrawal (2008), Warren & Visser (2016).
  *World Development* (2018) on Mexico's community forestry, itself a global
  success model, treats persistent elite capture as one of the field's
  daunting challenges and notes that the "model" framing hides internal
  political struggle.
- **External factors under-emphasised** (Agrawal 2002; Johnson 1997; Steins et
  al. 2000; Berkes 2002, 2005): community-based CPR analyses treat external
  forces as a black box; Berkes stresses cross-scale linkages.
- **Structure, not process** (Cox et al.'s own noted limitation): the
  principles capture structural attributes and not trust, legitimacy or
  transparency as processes.
- **Ostrom's own position on prescriptive status:** she clarified that the
  principles are **not a blueprint** and should not be applied in
  cookie-cutter fashion. Important for C5.2 — the mechanism explicitly
  declines to be a deployment plan.
- **Scale:** Shin et al. (2022), "How Ostrom's design principles apply to
  large-scale commons: cooperation over international river basins," *Review
  of Policy Research* — notes that empirical tests of the principles at large
  scale are **relatively few**. Araral (2014), "Ostrom, Hardin and the
  commons: a critical appreciation and a revisionist view."

## Theme 7 — Scale limits, and Ostrom's own statement of them

- **Ostrom (2009), "Polycentric systems for coping with climate change"**
  (World Bank Policy Research WP 5095) states plainly that it is much easier to
  craft solutions for collective-action problems at **smaller-scale common-pool
  resources than for the global commons**. This is the primary source for
  NEEC's scale disclosure: the limitation is the author's own, not a critic's.
- **Ostrom (2010), "Polycentric systems for coping with collective action and
  global environmental change,"** *Global Environmental Change* 20: 550–557;
  and **"Beyond Markets and States,"** *AER* 100(3): 641–672. Polycentric
  systems have multiple governing authorities at different scales rather than
  one monocentric unit, each exercising considerable independence within its
  domain (Ostrom 2010, p. 552).
- **Ostrom (2012):** relying entirely on international efforts to solve global
  climate problems needs rethinking; multilateral policy is **necessary but not
  sufficient**.
- **Stern (2011), "Design principles for global commons,"** *International
  Journal of the Commons*: applying principles 7 and 8 to global commons is
  **very difficult and can even be counterproductive**, because lower-level
  governments and firms have incentives to externalise degradation costs. His
  conclusion: the design principle **needs modification** for global resource
  commons — higher-level authorities should facilitate lower-level rule-making
  by delegating authority *and* supplying knowledge and resources.
- **Shin et al. (2022)**, international river basins: empirical tests of the
  design principles at large scale are relatively few.

**NEEC reading.** The scale question is not a hostile framing; it is the
mechanism's own stated boundary. A local CPR institution is the unit; NEEC's
thresholds are population-level. See the scoring document's stated scoring
convention for how the two are reconciled.

## Theme 8 — The distributional finding that matters most for Domain 1

**Chhatre & Agrawal (2009),** "Trade-offs and synergies between carbon storage
and livelihood benefits from forest commons," *PNAS* 106(42): 17667–17670
(edited and approved by Ostrom). Original data on **80 forest commons in 10
countries** across Asia, Africa and Latin America.
- Larger forest size **and** greater local rule-making autonomy are associated
  with high carbon storage *and* high livelihood benefits.
- **Ownership differences produce trade-offs:** government ownership carries a
  higher probability of overuse (low carbon storage, high livelihood benefits);
  **community ownership is associated with LOW livelihood benefits but HIGH
  carbon storage, because communities defer use.**
- Forests contribute to livelihoods for **more than half a billion users**.
- Contested: Ternström, Mukhopadhyay & Ghate (*PNAS* 2010, letter) challenge the
  definitions, variable choice and sample selection, and note high current
  livelihood benefit may degrade future carbon storage.

**NEEC reading — this is the single most consequential empirical finding in the
whole evaluation.** The mechanism's characteristic success mode is *resource
conservation achieved partly by deferring consumption*. That is close to the
opposite of a poverty-elimination or wealth-building mechanism. It supports a
strong Domain 4 ecological reading and a weak Domain 1 reading simultaneously,
and it is community-ownership-specific rather than a generic commons claim.

## Theme 9 — Equity within the commons: gender and marginalised users

- **Bina Agarwal (2001), "Participatory Exclusions, Community Forestry, and
  Gender,"** *World Development* 29(10): 1623–1648 — the standing concept:
  formal membership in a commons institution does not produce effective voice.
- **Agarwal (2009),** *Ecological Economics* 68(11): 2785–2799, and **(2010),**
  *World Development* 38(1): 98–112 ("Does women's proportional strength affect
  their participation?"): women's proportional strength in the governing body
  matters, and women's presence **improves forest protection and rule
  compliance**. Ostrom herself endorsed the book-length treatment (*Gender and
  Green Governance*, 2010).
- **Affirmative-action quotas** for women and other marginalised groups were
  introduced on executive committees of Indian Joint Forest Management user
  groups **from the 1990s** — evidence that the exclusion is recognised and
  that remedies exist inside the mechanism.
- Corroborating cases: Mexico (Vázquez García 2015 — gender discrimination in
  land tenure, gendered division of labour, unequal benefit distribution, and a
  strictly commercial view of management plans exclude women from community
  forest management); Tanzania (2020 — exclusion becomes normalised in everyday
  discourse *even in programmes that claim equal participation*).
- Nepal CFUG field study (2024, above): livelihood initiatives have most
  limited reach for **women, indigenous people, and landless users**.

**NEEC reading.** Two-sided. The mechanism has a documented internal-exclusion
problem that is structural, not incidental — and it has documented, working
remedies applied within the mechanism itself. That is a Partial, not a failure
and not a pass.

## Theme 10 — Knowledge and digital commons: the scope boundary

- Hess & Ostrom, *Understanding Knowledge as a Commons* (MIT Press, 2006/2007).
  Design principles have been applied to Wikipedia (Viégas et al. 2007; Forte
  et al. 2009), to the Drupal open-source community (Rozas), to open-data
  ecosystems (Linåker & Runeson 2022), and to data commons (Ruhaak et al.,
  2021).
- **But the Governing Knowledge Commons workshop's own position** is that
  knowledge resources may not be common-pool resources at all, that a tragedy
  of the commons may not be the key threat, and that Ostrom's design principles
  are **neither natural starting points nor natural conclusions** for shared
  knowledge resources.
- Linåker & Runeson (2022): natural commons must regulate *consumption*, while
  digital commons must *stimulate* both use and provisioning — subtractability
  concerns maintenance resources, not availability.

**NEEC reading — scope decision.** The digital/knowledge commons extension is
**adjacent, not included**, and is scored as a scenario, exactly as Islamic
finance's social-finance layer was in Session 22. The reason is stronger here:
the field's own leading workshop says the principles do not transfer
straightforwardly. Including it would change C1.4 and C5.5 materially, so the
scenario is enumerated rather than folded in.

## Theme 11 — Longevity evidence (for C5.1 and C3.3)

- **Valencia's Tribunal de las Aguas:** in continuous operation for roughly a
  millennium — most accounts place its origin in the Caliphate period (9th–10th
  century), with the first written reference in the 13th-century Valencian
  *Furs*; eight trustees, each elected by one of eight irrigation communities,
  meeting publicly every Thursday, with oral proceedings and unappealable
  rulings. **UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, 2009.** Reported never to
  have required recourse to the ordinary courts to settle a water-distribution
  dispute.
- **Törbel, Switzerland:** the alpine commons case that opens *Governing the
  Commons*; Ostrom's 1990 study brought it to international attention.
- **Japanese irrigation CPRs** and *iriai* forest commons; **Philippine
  *zanjera*** irrigation — Araral (2013) studies a 400-year-old *zanjera*.

**NEEC reading.** This is the strongest longevity evidence of any system in the
corpus by an order of magnitude — centuries to a millennium of continuous
operation, with documented outcomes. It bears directly on C5.1 and on C3.3's
multi-failure resistance. It is also the evidence most exposed to the
selection-on-the-dependent-variable problem (Theme 12).

## Theme 12 — The central methodological disclosure (flagged in D7)

The design principles were **derived from cases that survived**. Ostrom's
sample was long-enduring CPR institutions; the principles are what those
institutions had in common. Cox et al. (2010) partially answer this by
examining 91 studies including failures, and the Ostrom Workshop states the
principles are usually *absent* in failed cases — which is the right test. But
the founding derivation is still selection on the dependent variable, and the
survivorship problem is not fully dissolved by a later literature that largely
tests the same principles on similar case types.

Ostrom's own guard: the principles are **not a blueprint** and should not be
applied cookie-cutter. Handled under Appendix H.6.
