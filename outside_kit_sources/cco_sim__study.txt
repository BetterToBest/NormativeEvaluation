Health financing, prices and what the results depend on

The math and code of this simulation were engineered by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, from the concepts in the author's book Better To Best and his related vision for eradicating extreme poverty while enriching cultures and supporting human flourishing. The model has not yet been reviewed by an independent economist; the full code is open for anyone to check, and expert collaborators are welcome.

The 500-seed study, run on a rented server, ran the same 500 simulated populations (500 adults each, 20 years) with and without Compassionism under three health systems and three economies, and then asked which inputs the answer depends on. Every comparison is paired: the same people and the same luck in both runs. Figures are the study's, copied from its results report.

Basket poverty under three health systems #

Compassionism lowers basket poverty in every combination of health system and economy. In Reference under today's health system it falls by 31.7 points (Adverse 38.4, Stress 14.3). Single payer lowers poverty before Compassionism starts, so Compassionism's own drop is 2.4 points smaller under it in Reference, and the level reached with both is the lowest of the three settings.

The safety net's outlays are today's programs, paid in both runs; they are not program cost. The model does not reconcile tax revenue with these outlays: it reports them, it does not show that they are financed. Under A the household health bill is premiums and out-of-pocket costs. Single payer depends on the Yale preprint #

The single-payer savings are inputs from the Yale preprint, not results of this model. The study ran the preprint's central case and two others. In the cautious case, savings are cut; in the high-cost case providers are paid near commercial rates, so the savings vanish and only the added care remains. Single payer still lowers poverty with the cautious savings. In the high-cost case the plan costs more per person than today's system, so any gain there comes from the financing rule, not from savings.

The price channel #

The model raises essentials' prices when more of them are bought, and the program's spending also raises the general price level. With prices as released, "too little wealth" rises with Compassionism (Reference: +12.0 points). With no price response at all it falls instead (26.1 points lower in Reference), and falls in every environment. So the rise comes through the price response: program inflation erodes cash savings, so more adults end with too little real wealth even though their basket is covered. Making the responses stronger changes little, so what matters is whether the program's inflation happens, not its strength. How strongly prices respond to benefit-funded demand is not settled.

What the results depend on #

Two methods ask which inputs the answer depends on. Screening (the Morris method, 1991) ranks how far each input moves an outcome over its range, on 20 random paths of 20 paired runs (seeds) each. The follow-up Sobol run (the Sobol method, Saltelli et al. 2010) splits the spread of each outcome among the inputs, with 128 base points and 32 paired runs (seeds) per point, in Stress. Participation and the amount of BU paid explain most of the spread in poverty, BLEI poverty, cost and inflation. These are design choices about the program's size, not uncertain facts about the world. For basket poverty, the income effect on earnings is the one uncertain behavioral constant that matters. "Too little wealth" depends most on the share of extra cash people spend.

Each bar is the share of the spread in one outcome that an input explains, alone and together with the others (the Sobol total index, Stress). A longer bar means the answer depends more on that input. The line on each bar is its 95% interval. Pick an outcome to redraw the chart; every number is in the table below it.

Tested against history: 2008 and 2020 #

A separate check, run on the same model: it was put through a scripted 2008 to 2012 recession and through 2020 and 2021, and compared with what happened. The seven comparisons below, and what would count as a match, were fixed before the model was run, and no constant was adjusted to fit the past. The check reads changes, not levels. The model's adults are built from 2022 data, so it asks how a population like today's would respond to a shock like 2008 or 2020, not whether it reproduces those years. The model's figure is the rise in basket poverty (adults below the cost of living) with today's safety net, the shock run minus the same run with no recession, over 500 paired seeds; the data's is the change from the year before the shock. In short: the direction is right (poverty rises in both shocks, as in the data), the size is too big (a rise of +6.86 points at the model's peak against +2.6 in the official rate) and the peak comes too late. Verdicts: matches 2; same direction, different size 2; does not match 1; reported with no verdict 2.

The clearest miss is the safety net. In the data the safety net absorbed about 73% of the recession's rise in poverty (a rise of +5.77 points before taxes and transfers, +1.56 after). In the model it absorbs about 0.5% (a rise of +6.90 points without the safety net, +6.86 with it). The file's label is "same direction, different size", because the model's share is just above zero and the rule fixed in advance gives that label to any positive share; the gap is far outside the band of 20 points, so in substance it is a miss. Three reasons, all in the model's design: (a) SNAP and the tax credits are paid on last year's income, so they arrive a year late; (b) the model's cost-of-living line is high, and benefits of a few thousand dollars seldom lift someone over it (they shrink the gap instead); (c) the data's "before transfers" removes far more than the model's three programs (SNAP, the Earned Income Tax Credit and the refundable Child Tax Credit): it also removes unemployment insurance, Social Security, housing aid and the tax system, while in the model unemployment insurance is in both runs and the others do not respond to the shock. So in a downturn the model's baseline safety net is weaker than the real one, and a comparison of Compassionism against today's safety net in a recession should be read with that in mind. The model has no stimulus payments or weekly unemployment top-up, which is why the 2020 supplemental rate (reported, no verdict) fell in the data and rose in the model. The data are outside figures, not verified against Census, BLS or the Federal Reserve by this repository. Limits and what is still open #

All single-payer results rest on the Yale preprint's savings. The model does not reconcile tax revenue with today's safety-net outlays or with government's share of the plan. The price response drives the "too little wealth" result and is not calibrated to outside evidence. The cost-of-living line is the unweighted state average. The work rules of SNAP (food stamps) and a separate Child Tax Credit take-up are not modeled. The community businesses' (PTF) health-sector cut under single payer is an upper reading. The model has not yet been reviewed by an independent economist.
