> This criterion asks whether participants can build a $70,000+/20-year household buffer through the system's own mechanism. Georgism's own C1.2a write-up (this corpus, Session 6) explicitly distinguishes its own Citizen's Dividend — "a direct annual pass-through of that year's land-rent surplus" — from "a growing, held corpus the way **Alaska's actual Permanent Fund** (which invests its principal and distributes only a portion of returns)... are," naming this exact system as the stronger alternative it is not itself claiming to be. This evaluation is the first opportunity to test that implied claim directly, and Appendix H.7v2's own worked anchor for this exact band answers it precisely: the 0.5 band is defined as "some accumulation mechanism exists but is narrow, indirect, or **held collectively rather than individually** — real, but not clearly clearing the household-buffer bar as stated," illustrated there by Degrowth's community land trusts and cooperatives. A sovereign wealth fund fits this description at least as well as that anchor case, and arguably more purely: Norway's fund is real, large ($2+ trillion), continuously growing, and professionally invested — not a flow that is captured and immediately redistributed, the way Georgism's dividend or a bare UBI payment are (both correctly scored 0.0 here on exactly that reasoning) — but no citizen holds any individually titled, transferable, or even notionally severable claim on it. Research located for this evaluation states this distinction directly: each Norwegian's roughly $360,000–400,000 notional per-capita share is described in multiple 2026 sources as "a claim on a collective asset, not a bank balance" that "nobody can withdraw." Alaska's dividend itself does not independently support a higher score — it is consumed as ordinary income, matching Georgism's and UBI's own reasoning — so the 0.5 here rests on the fund itself, not the payout. *(The section continues at the link.)*

Norway itself is the clearest illustration of the resulting limits: income inequality there remains among the world's lowest, but wealth concentration at the top is real and, per a March 2026 analysis, rising — just ten people in Norway now own more wealth than the bottom 50% of the population combined, entirely outside anything the fund itself touches, since the fund is a public asset that leaves private equity and business ownership untouched. Alaska's own measured effect on income distribution is small and precisely estimated: a 0.02 reduction in the Gini coefficient (Berman, 2024) — real, but modest. *(The section continues at the link.)*
