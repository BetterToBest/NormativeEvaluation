**Close call.** Scored 0.0; a reasonable scorer could set 0.5. The housing plank's pressure on land and rental values

**Published (v1) rationale**, under the heading "C1.2b Prevention of Exploitative Accumulation: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as a genuinely contestable score" at [NEEC_UniversalBasicServices_scoring_scratch.md, line 153](https://github.com/BetterToBest/NormativeEvaluation/blob/main/NEEC_UniversalBasicServices_scoring_scratch.md?plain=1#L153):

> This criterion asks whether the system structurally prevents wealth *concentration* — a Gini-coefficient-relevant question, distinct from C1.2a's resilience-buffer question. This is the single most consequential call in this evaluation, and it was revised once during drafting, disclosed here rather than silently settled. **The case for 0.5** (this evaluation's initial read): the shelter sector's plan to double the UK's social housing stock by 1.5 million units removes that many households from the private rental market entirely, a real, costed, committed mechanism — unlike Doughnut Economics, which was scored 0.0 here specifically for citing compatible redistributive mechanisms without adopting any of its own, UBS *does* adopt a specific mechanism of its own, which seemed to warrant credit above Doughnut Economics' clean zero, on the model of Georgism's own 0.5 for land value capture. **Why this evaluation lands at 0.0 instead:** on closer comparison with Georgism's actual mechanism, the analogy does not hold. Georgism's land value tax *captures wealth that would otherwise accumulate to landowners* — it is a device that removes an existing, ongoing accumulation from one party's hands. UBS's shelter sector does the opposite kind of thing: it *builds new housing and gives it away at zero rent*, funded by general taxation. This is real and valuable, but it does not capture, cap, or redistribute any existing stock of wealth; it is an expenditure commitment, not a wealth- concentration-prevention mechanism, and — like every other part of the proposal — it says nothing about the Gini coefficient of the population's existing asset holdings. The proposal's funding mechanism (reducing the Personal Allowance) is a tax-and-transfer adjustment, not a wealth tax, asset cap, or land-value-capture device either. On reflection, this evaluation concludes UBS has no C1.2b mechanism of the kind this criterion asks about, at any scale — a different, and weaker, case than either Georgism's or Mutual Credit/LETS's own targeted mechanisms. *(The section continues at the link.)*

> UBS's shelter sector is one of its most concretely specified components: 1.5 million new social housing units, zero rent on a needs basis, financed through 30-year Treasury bonds, exempted from Council Tax, and including a utilities allowance. The closest full-scale real-world analogue is Vienna's century-old social and cooperative housing system — roughly 40–60% of Viennese residents live in municipally-owned or subsidized cooperative housing (depending on how subsidized cooperative units are counted alongside directly municipal ones), average rents run well below comparable European capitals (on the order of €10.5/m² against London's more than triple that figure), and the system traces continuously to the "Red Vienna" municipal housing program begun in 1919. This is real, genuine, multi-generational evidence that decommodified housing at population scale is buildable and durable. Against this, two real countervailing findings: first, independent critical assessments of Vienna (including from the American Enterprise Institute and the Warsaw Enterprise Institute, both writing from a skeptical, market-oriented starting point) document that Vienna's own system, at scale, has drifted toward disproportionately benefiting long-tenured, better-off households under comparatively light means-testing — up to half of higher-income Viennese households reportedly live in subsidized housing, and entry costs for newer cooperative-unit residents (an upfront payment on the order of $38,000, and rent roughly 17% higher than long-tenured neighbors in some cases) are real and non-trivial. Second, the specific "zero rent on a needs basis" design UBS itself proposes has never been implemented at the scale proposed anywhere; Vienna's own system charges modest, not zero, rents, funded via a dedicated payroll-style tax. *(The section continues at the link.)*

> Unlike Doughnut Economics — which names automation as a concern but supplies no operational response and consequently fails this criterion outright — UBS has a genuine, if partial, mechanism. > NEEC's own threshold asks for "unconditional provision covering 100% of basic needs (housing, food, healthcare, utilities) for all residents" — language that maps almost directly onto UBS's own named sectors. Several UBS sectors genuinely clear an unconditional-access bar for the whole population (universal healthcare and education already function this way in the UK; free transport and basic information access are proposed as flatly universal, with no means test). This is a real, substantive design difference from a clean unconditional-baseline system. *(The section continues at the link.)*
