## 1. Purpose, inputs and outputs

### 1.1 What the protocol is for

1. An independent scorer, given the same evidence, reaches the same score.
2. Anyone can add a new system without drift in standards.
3. A replication audit can say precisely where it agrees or disagrees, at the
   level of the single criterion.
4. Every claim a scoring document makes about the rest of the corpus is
   checked by machine, and stays true as the corpus grows.

The first three are Appendix H.1's; the fourth is new in v2.

### 1.2 What a scorer needs

| File | What it is |
|---|---|
| `SCORING_PROTOCOL.md` | this document |
| `criteria.json` | the 26 criteria: definitions (Paper Section 6; C1.2a, C1.2b and C1.5 from Section 12.3) and the 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.0 anchors (Appendix H.7 and H.7v2), generated from Paper v1.4 by `build_criteria.py`, which applies the corrections of 4.6 and records each in the file; the criteria's source of truth from Paper v2.0 on (D19); schema in `criteria_schema.json` |
| `neec_scores.csv` | the canonical corpus, one row per system |
| `neec_corpus.json` | the corpus as one JSON file: each entry's key, code, display name, scope class and 26 scores, generated by `build_corpus_file.py` and published as the corpus's dataset (decisions D23, D27(a)) |
| `summary_block_schema.json` | the schema of the summary block every scoring document ends with (section 9) |
| `neec_entry.py` | the generic entry verifier: validates a summary block and computes the enumeration, the tier-robustness measure and the peer matrix |
| Appendix B of this document | the clauses of each multi-clause Pass Threshold (decision D28) |

For a blind replication (section 11), the corpus is supplied as
`neec_corpus.json` with the target withheld, and this protocol as a blind copy
(decision D24).

### 1.3 What a scorer produces

One Markdown scoring document per system (section 7), ending in one summary
block (section 9). Insertion into the canonical corpus is a separate pass run
by the maintainers (section 10).

## 2. Scale, arithmetic and tiers

### 2.1 The scale

Each criterion receives exactly one of three values:

- **1.0 — Pass.** The system robustly and structurally satisfies the
  criterion's Pass Threshold, under normal conditions and, where the criterion
  specifies one, under stress. This is a claim that the threshold is cleared,
  not a claim of perfection.
- **0.5 — Partial / Conditional.** A genuine, non-trivial mechanism that does
  not clear the threshold robustly: the performance falls short of the
  threshold's figure, or holds only under favourable or untested conditions,
  or covers only part of the relevant population, or the sources are
  genuinely ambiguous (in which case the score is flagged, 6.1).
- **0.0 — Structural Failure.** No structural mechanism addresses the
  criterion; or the system excludes the criterion's concern as illegitimate;
  or the best evidence shows performance far below the threshold with no
  credible pathway to close the gap under the system's own logic.

Two decisions refine the 0.0 band. **D29** (4.1) says what counts as "the
system's own logic" when implementations of a system have failed, and when a
remedy is the system's own. **D26** narrows the band for one criterion: C3.2
reserves 0.0 for an active inflationary mechanism with no counterbalancing
element, and scores the absence of any inflation-control mechanism 0.5 (4.6).

Appendix H.2 calls the 0.0 band "Full Failure"; every Step 1b document heads it
"Structural Failure", which matches the tier definition (2.5). The two are
synonyms; text searches should use both.

### 2.2 Reason continuously, then round once

For each criterion, first form a continuous or ranged estimate of where the
system's actual or credibly projected performance falls against the Pass
Threshold, grounded in the best available evidence, and write it down
("Estimated performance"). Only then round it to the nearest valid value. Where
the estimate straddles a boundary, state the range and say so; that is also the
first sign that the call may need a flag (6.1). Rounding happens once, at the
criterion level. Nothing above the criterion is re-estimated, re-rounded,
weighted or averaged (Appendix H.3).

### 2.3 The operative threshold, clause by clause (decision D28, revision R4)

Score against the criterion's **Pass Threshold** as `criteria.json` gives it
(`definition.pass_threshold`, which each anchor's threshold line reproduces
verbatim, 4.6), not against its Requirement line where the two differ (C1.1
states an aspirational 95% requirement and an operative 90% / 85% threshold).
If you find another such gap, flag it rather than silently choosing.

Twenty-one criteria have a Pass Threshold of more than one clause; Appendix B
splits each into its clauses, verbatim and in order. Five have one clause
(C1.2a, C1.2b, C1.5, C2.2, C3.3).

- **(a) Clause by clause.** Where a Pass Threshold has several clauses, 1.0
  requires every clause to be shown cleared on its own estimate (2.2). The
  criterion's section states an estimate for each clause, in Appendix B's
  order.
- **(b) Silence is not clearance.** A clause the document does not estimate
  cannot support a 1.0.
- **(c) Reach.** For a mechanism, a clause whose quantity the mechanism does
  not govern even when generalised cannot be credited (3.2).
- **(d) Method clauses.** A clause that names how a condition is evidenced
  ("validated through revealed preference", "validated through modeling") is
  satisfied by evidence at least as strong as the method named. Observed
  operation at the relevant scale is at least as strong as modelling.
- **(e) Moot clauses.** A clause whose condition cannot apply to the entry, as
  its document establishes, does not block a 1.0 — a transition, scale-up or
  coordination the document shows is not needed. The same reading is applied
  to every entry of the same class.
- **(f) 0.0 is unchanged.** 2.1's 0.0 conditions apply to the criterion as a
  whole. A clause-level gap alone never makes 0.0, so this rule cannot change
  a failure count or a tier.
- **(g) Anchors describe; thresholds govern** (4.6).

A criterion whose clauses are not all shown cleared scores 0.5, unless 2.1's
0.0 conditions hold for the criterion as a whole. The audit that led to this
rule, its reasons, the alternatives weighed and the textual bound on what it
can change are in `NEEC_R4_MultiClause_Audit_s35.md`.

### 2.4 Arithmetic

- **Domain 1** (Material Security) is the sum of six criteria — C1.1, C1.2a,
  C1.2b, C1.3, C1.4, C1.5 — maximum 6.
- **Domains 2 to 5** are each the sum of five criteria, maximum 5.
- **Total** is the sum of the five domains, maximum 26.
- **Percent** is Total / 26 × 100, displayed as a whole number (decision
  D21), as in `neec_scores.csv`, the Paper and the Report. Totals move in
  0.5-point steps, about 1.9 percentage points apart, so no two distinct
  totals share a whole percent, and none falls exactly on a half, so the
  rounding is never ambiguous. A domain's percent is displayed the same way.
  Enumeration shares (6.3, 6.4) keep one decimal place.
- **Failures** is the number of criteria scored exactly 0.0.

Every stated domain total shows its addends (Appendix H.4), for example
`Domain 4 = 0.5 + 0.0 + 0.5 + 0.0 + 0.0 = 1.0/5 (20%)`.

### 2.5 Tiers

| Tier | Failures |
|---|---|
| Potentially Adequate | 0 to 2 |
| Partially Adequate | 3 to 5 |
| Structurally Inadequate | 6 or more |

The tier depends on the failure count only, never on the total, so two systems
with the same total can sit in different tiers. A 0.5 is never a failure,
however many a system has (Appendix H.5).

## 3. Scope declaration (decision D8)

### 3.1 The three scope classes

Every entry declares exactly one scope class before scoring.

- **Mechanism** — an institution, instrument or policy family that operates
  inside a wider economy without itself specifying that economy. Declared
  examples: Georgism / Land Value Tax, Mutual Credit / LETS, Sovereign Wealth
  Fund Statism, Islamic finance, Ostrom-style commons governance (the
  "narrow single-mechanism" archetype).
- **Configured national economy** — an existing or historical national
  political economy, scored as configured at a stated date. Declared examples:
  State Capitalism / China, / Singapore and / Qatar (the "configured national
  political economy" archetype).
- **Comprehensive system** — a design or vision for a whole economy, scored as
  its own sources specify it.

**Assigned classes (decision D17).** Fifteen entries scored before this
protocol declare no class. The project owner assigned them in Session 28, and
their summary blocks record the class with basis `assigned`:

- Configured national economy: Status Quo Market Capitalism, Nordic Social Democracy, Centrally Planned Socialism.
- Mechanism: MMT + Job Guarantee, Universal Basic Income, Universal Basic Services.
- Comprehensive system: Market Socialism, Libertarian Minarchism, Degrowth Economics, Stakeholder Capitalism, Fully Automated Luxury Communism, Participatory Economics, CCO-PTF-CIP-SZH, Integral, Doughnut Economics.

The closest calls were Status Quo Market Capitalism (scored mainly from US
data), Stakeholder Capitalism (a governance reform, or a variant of
capitalism) and Universal Basic Services (a federation of mechanisms). No
score changed on assignment.

Within each class, entries are grouped into **archetypes** (5.1), which decide
the peers an entry is calibrated against.

### 3.2 How population-scope thresholds are applied

Many thresholds refer to "the population", "participants" or "residents".
The protocol, not the scorer, decides how they are read:

- **Configured national economy:** everyone who lives and works in the
  economy, non-citizens included. A migrant or non-resident workforce that the
  configuration depends on is part of the configuration (the Singapore and
  Qatar precedents). Narrower populations — citizens only; citizens and
  permanent residents — are scenarios (6.5): never scores, never flags.
- **Mechanism: generalisation, not best-casing.** Score the mechanism as
  though it governed every part of an economy it could govern, then apply the
  population-scope thresholds to the resulting economy-wide population. Credit
  a country's outcomes to the mechanism only where the mechanism does the work
  ("score the mechanism, not a country"). Whether an adjacent instrument or
  extension lies inside the mechanism's boundary is settled by the extension
  test below; the other reading is a scenario.
- **Comprehensive system:** the population the system's own sources claim to
  serve, ordinarily a whole society. Where the sources are ambiguous about who
  is covered, apply the ambiguity rule (4.2): the ambiguity becomes a flagged
  call, not a change of scope.

**Reach (decision D28(c)).** For a mechanism, a clause of a Pass Threshold
whose quantity the mechanism does not govern even when generalised cannot be
credited, since crediting it would credit the host economy. Such a clause holds
the criterion at 0.5 at most (2.3); it does not by itself make the criterion
0.0.

**Extensions (decision D31, revision R6).** An institution, programme or
domain beyond a mechanism's core cases belongs to the scored mechanism when the
mechanism's own literature — its founding works and the scholarship that
develops them — makes it part of the mechanism's design explicitly: by pairing
it with the mechanism in the design itself, or by applying the mechanism's
defining rules or principles to it one by one. It is scored out when that
literature does neither, or when it disputes that the design transfers — a
dispute within the mechanism's own literature, as distinct from critics'
doubts that the mechanism works. An extension scored out is reported as a
scope scenario counting it in; an extension scored in, as a scope scenario
counting it out wherever that would change a score. The document cites the
source that decides each extension; the scorer's own mapping, however careful,
is argument rather than that source (4.3). The corpus's precedents, as their
documents decide them: Georgism's citizen's dividend (paired with the tax in
the design scored: in); Islamic finance's social-finance layer (not part of
the profit-sharing contract set in that literature: out, with a scenario);
Ostrom-style commons governance's knowledge and digital commons (the transfer
disputed within the knowledge-commons literature: out, with a scenario) and
its community land trusts (in, with a scenario counting them out; the source
this rule asks for is confirmed in the rescoring pass, section 13).

**Remedies from outside the scored system (decision D29(c)).** A remedy
supplied from outside the entry's declared scope — for a mechanism, an
instrument that the extension test places beyond its boundary; for a
comprehensive system, one its own sources do not specify; for a configured
national economy, one that is not part of the configuration at its stated
date — is not credited to the entry. Where it would change a score, a scope
scenario counts it in (4.1).

### 3.3 Scope is decided first, and stated

The document states its class and population rule in its first section
(7.2), and the summary block records them (`scope`). A scope decision that needs
confirmation is marked as such and confirmed before the scores are fixed —
by the project owner until Session 35 and under the owner's delegation since
(section 13) — as it was in the Singapore, Qatar, Islamic finance and Ostrom
evaluations.

### 3.4 Scope is not a flag

A flag records doubt about a score *given* the declared scope. A different
scope is a scenario. The same question must not appear as both.

**The scope-or-doubt test (decision D18(b), revision R1).** A flag's
alternative reading is a **scope question** when it (i) reads the criterion's
threshold against a population, domain or frame other than the one the
declared population rule fixes — for a mechanism, the resource or membership it
governs in place of the economy-wide population — or (ii) counts an adjacent
instrument, programme or extension in or out of the mechanism's boundary
(3.2). A scope question is reported as a scenario (6.5) and leaves the
register. An alternative reading that joins such a clause to a doubt stays a
flag if the doubt alone reaches the alternative value, and stays a flag when
the text does not settle whether it does.

**Scope questions carried as flags before this test.** Two entries scored
before the test carried scope questions among their flags.

- (a) Singapore's C1.5 flag ("citizen-and-PR-only population scope") was also
  part of its citizens-and-permanent-residents scenario. It is removed from the
  flag register and stays in the scenario: 11 flags, 2,048 combinations, the
  reach and the enumeration share unchanged, the point span 6.0 -> 5.5
  (decision D18(a), Session 28).
- (b) Most of Ostrom-style commons governance's twenty flags, on its own
  document's account, turned on one question — whether a mechanism that
  governs a resource should be read against criteria that ask about a
  population. Sorting them waited for the first pilot, which supplied an
  independent check (section 11). The test above, applied to each flag's own
  stated alternative reading (`d18b_reexpression_s32.py`), moved four flags —
  C1.3, C3.1, C3.5 and C4.2 — into scope scenarios and kept two borderline
  flags, C4.3 and C5.2; no score changed (decision D18(b), decided in Session
  32 and applied in Session 33, with D26).

### 3.5 Worked example: Ostrom-style commons governance

The entry declares the mechanism class, with "generalisation, not
best-casing" as its population rule. As first scored, its two stated joint
readings — the long-enduring case (19.5/26, 0 failures) and strict population
scope (9.5/26, 11 failures) — reached all three tiers: the scope question
expressed in NEEC's own units, through the flag register. Under this protocol
the rule for the scored reading is fixed rather than chosen, and the scope
questions are scenarios (3.4(b)). The entry now stands at 14.5/26 with 3
failures, Partially Adequate; its joint readings are the two extremes of its
fifteen flags — every call upward, 18.0/26 with 0 failures, and every call
downward, 10.5/26 with 9 failures — which still reach all three tiers; and
three scope scenarios are reported but not scored: the knowledge and digital
commons counted in (15.5/26, 2 failures, Potentially Adequate), community land
trusts counted out (14.0/26, 4 failures, Partially Adequate), and the
thresholds read against the governed resource and its members (16.0/26, 3
failures, Partially Adequate). A scenario may cross a tier, as the first does;
it is still not scored.

## 4. Evidence standards

### 4.1 Evidentiary tier by type of system (Appendix H.6)

- **Real-world implementations:** score from documented outcomes. A 1.0 or a
  0.0 should be traceable to a specific figure or well-established finding,
  not to reputation.
- **Historical or defunct systems:** score from the historical record, treating
  the actual trajectory, collapse included, as evidence.
- **Component-validated theoretical systems:** score the claimed mechanism, but
  calibrate confidence by the track record of the real-world components it
  cites — and treat each component's record as evidence only for the criteria
  it bears on.
- **Purely theoretical systems:** apply the same threshold reasoning, and let
  Domain 5 reflect the absence of deployment evidence honestly.

**Implementation failure and the system's own logic (decision D29, revision
R2).** 2.1 places at 0.0 performance far below the threshold "with no credible
pathway to close the gap under the system's own logic". For that test:

- **(a)** A failure documented in several independent implementations of the
  scored system — in different places, periods or hands — is evidence about
  the system's own logic, not a contingent shortfall of implementation, unless
  the document shows, from the system's own sources, that the failing
  implementations lacked or violated a feature the system requires. A single
  failed implementation is evidence of the same kind, but weaker; the document
  weighs it and says how.
- **(b)** A credible pathway is one the system provides: a rule, instrument or
  correction mechanism its own sources name, or, for a configured national
  economy, one in its configuration at the stated date. The expectation that
  later implementations will avoid a failure the earlier ones replicated is not
  a pathway.
- **(c)** A remedy supplied from outside the entry's declared scope is not
  credited to it (3.2); where it would change a score, a scope scenario counts
  it in.
- **(d)** Whether a remedy inside the scope works — in substance, not in form
  only — is a question of evidence, scored under this section and flagged
  where the evidence is divided (6.1).

### 4.2 Ambiguous source material

(1) State the interpretations the sources could support. (2) State which one
the preponderance of the text supports, with the evidence. (3) Score against
that reading. (4) State what the score would be under the alternative — that
is, flag it (6.1) with the alternative score named. The reference example is
Integral's C2.2 (Paper Appendix E): "unconditional essentials" would score 1.0,
"contribution-dependent" 0.0, and the text supports the middle reading, 0.5.

### 4.3 Citation practice

Cite a specific source, figure or quotation wherever one exists. Where a score
rests on estimation, say so — "estimated", "projected". Where sources conflict,
disclose the range instead of silently picking one figure.

### 4.4 The spread of sources

Draw on the founding literature, peer-reviewed empirical work, real-world
implementations at scale, and deliberately balanced critical sources from
more than one ideological direction. State the source mix in the document's
overview.

### 4.5 Evidence that will date

Name the figures that will date and what a later review should re-check.

### 4.6 Anchors (decisions D26, D28(g))

`criteria.json` gives every criterion's 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.0 anchors with examples
from the corpus, and `build_criteria.py` checks every example against the
published scores. **Anchors describe; thresholds govern:** where an anchor's
wording and the Pass Threshold differ, the Pass Threshold governs (2.3). Since
draft.5 each anchor's threshold line is the definition's Pass Threshold,
verbatim. `build_criteria.py` replaced 25 of the 26 Appendix H.7 threshold
lines; six had departed from their definitions in substance — C2.1, C2.2,
C2.5, C3.1, C5.1 and C5.3 — and the rest in wording only. Every change is
recorded in the file (`source.corrections`), and Paper v1.4's wording stays in
the pinned `criteria_s35_snapshot.json`. A scoring document quotes the
definition, not an abridgement.

**C3.2 (decision D26, revision R3).** The 0.0 band is reserved for an active
inflationary mechanism with no counterbalancing design element; a system that
specifies no inflation-control mechanism of its own, or has no monetary
function, scores 0.5, and the 0.5 band now says so. The band's stale count of
the corpus (Correction 7) is removed.

Two examples are wrong and carry a correction note in the file: **C4.3's 0.0
example** (Status Quo Market Capitalism is published at 0.5) and **C4.5's 1.0
example** (the quotation is Degrowth Economics' C4.5 text; Participatory
Economics is published at 0.0). Do not use either as an exemplar. Both will be
corrected in Paper v2.0.

## 5. Peer calibration before scores are fixed (decisions D9, D30)

### 5.1 Declare peers

Before fixing the vector, name the entries it should be calibrated against,
by rule (decision D30, revision R5): every entry of the same archetype in the
table below; where that archetype has fewer than three other members, every
entry of the same scope class as well; and any entry whose score the draft
relies on as a precedent ("the Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism precedent", "the
Nordic analogy"). Record them in the summary block's `peers` list. An entry
declares its scope class first (3.1), then its archetype by the tests below;
an entry that fits none proposes a new archetype, and the maintainers add it
here.

| Archetype | Scope class | Test | Members | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Configured national political economy | configured national economy | an existing or historical national economy, scored as configured at a stated date | Status Quo Market Capitalism, Nordic Social Democracy, Centrally Planned Socialism, State Capitalism / China, State Capitalism / Singapore, State Capitalism / Qatar | named in Step 1b; the first three by their class (D17) |
| Narrow single mechanism | mechanism | one institution or instrument, with the rules that make it work, layered onto a wider economy that it does not itself specify | MMT + Job Guarantee, Universal Basic Income, Georgism / Land Value Tax, Mutual Credit / LETS, Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism, Islamic Finance / Profit-Sharing Banking, Ostrom-Style Commons Governance | named in Step 1b; the first two assigned (D30) |
| Federation of committed sector mechanisms | mechanism | several committed mechanisms, each for one sector or need, adopted together without a design for the whole economy | Universal Basic Services | named in Step 1b |
| Comprehensive design with committed mechanisms | comprehensive system | a design for a whole economy whose sources commit to the institutions that deliver its outcomes: how production is owned, how resources are allocated, how income or provision is distributed | Market Socialism, Libertarian Minarchism, Degrowth Economics, Stakeholder Capitalism, Fully Automated Luxury Communism, Participatory Economics, CCO-PTF-CIP-SZH, Integral | assigned (D30) |
| Comprehensive vision without committed mechanisms | comprehensive system | a vision for a whole economy whose sources set goals or principles and decline to commit to the institutions that would deliver them | Doughnut Economics | named in Step 1b |

The closest calls in the assignment were MMT + Job Guarantee (one committed
mechanism with its financing doctrine; read as two mechanisms, a federation),
Degrowth Economics and Fully Automated Luxury Communism (their sources commit
to platforms of proposals rather than one integrated design; read as declining
to commit, they would join Doughnut Economics), and Stakeholder Capitalism (a
governance reform within capitalism, whose class D17 assigned). No score
changes with an archetype. Session 17's distinction between flow-based and
stock-based mechanisms describes the narrow single mechanisms; it does not
define peers.

### 5.2 Print the peer matrix

`python3 neec_entry.py --candidate DRAFT.md` (candidate mode, 9.3) prints the
draft vector beside the declared peers — or, if none are declared, beside its
three nearest neighbours by number of differing criteria — marks every
difference, and gives each peer's scope class.

### 5.3 Reconcile every difference

Where the draft differs from a peer whose evidence on that criterion looks
similar, either state in the criterion's rationale why the entry differs, or
flag the call (6.1) with the peer's value as its alternative. Calibration is
for consistency, not conformity: a justified difference stands.

### 5.4 Then fix the scores

The matrix is printed again at insertion (section 10).

## 6. Uncertainty: flags, joint readings, enumeration, tier robustness, scenarios

### 6.1 Flagged calls

**When to flag.** A criterion is a genuinely close call — a careful second
scorer could defensibly land on another value — or the sources are ambiguous
(4.2), or a peer difference was not resolved (5.3).

**What a flag records.** The criterion; the scored value; the alternative
score or scores, **named**; a short label for the alternative reading; and, in
the criterion's own section, the evidence on each side. The criterion heading
ends with " — flagged as contestable".

**Tracking flags.** When one criterion's score follows another's by rule — a
narrowed C1.5 that tracks C1.2a, for example — the second is a tracking flag:
it takes its alternative exactly when the call it tracks takes its own, and the
two count as one call.

**Counting.** "Flags" counts flagged criteria; "calls" counts independent
calls, which is flags minus tracking flags.

**A flag is never a scope question** (3.4).

**Documents written before this rule (decision D16).** Where an older document
flags a 0.5, argues the evidence in both directions and names no alternative,
the flag is encoded with both neighbours, [0.0, 1.0] (basis
`both-directions`); where it names only a direction, with the single value in
that direction (basis `direction-stated`). Three flags are encoded this way:
the C4.3 flags of Georgism, Mutual Credit / LETS and Sovereign Wealth Fund
Statism. The consequence, disclosed in the two documents concerned: under this
encoding Georgism's tier is not robust (C4.3 resolved to 0.0 gives 3 failures,
Partially Adequate), and Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism's all-downward reading
reaches Structurally Inadequate. The encoding not adopted — leaving such flags
out of the enumeration — would keep both tier-robust only by omitting a value
the document itself says a different reasoner could defensibly reach.

### 6.2 Joint readings (decision D6)

Flags are seldom independent: several often turn on one underlying question.
Where they do, state **coherent joint readings** — named resolutions of the
shared questions, at minimum the most favourable and the least favourable
coherent one — beside the reading as scored. A joint reading resolves only
flagged criteria, each to one of its stated alternatives, and moves tracking
flags with their calls. **The joint readings, not the enumeration, are the
informative summary of an entry's uncertainty;** the enumeration bounds them.

**Default (decision D16).** An entry that states no joint readings takes the two
extremes of its flag register as its joint readings — every call resolved
upward, and every call resolved downward — as the China, Singapore and Qatar
evaluations did. An entry with no flags has one reading: as scored.

### 6.3 The exhaustive enumeration

Every combination of calls — each call at its scored value or any of its
alternatives, tracking flags following — is enumerated. Report the number of
combinations, the tiers reached (with counts), the ranges of totals and
failures, and the share of combinations that keep the scored tier.
`neec_entry.py` computes all of these exactly. The enumeration treats calls as
independent, which D6 exists to deny; it is reported because it bounds the
joint readings, not as a probability.

### 6.4 Tier robustness (decision D13)

**Definition.** An entry's tier robustness is measured on its joint readings
(6.2):

- **reach** — the set of tiers its joint readings fall in;
- **span** — the difference between its highest and lowest joint-reading
  totals (points), and between its highest and lowest failure counts
  (failures).

An entry is **tier-robust** when every joint reading falls in its scored tier.

**Comparing entries.** Entry X is less tier-robust than entry Y when X's reach
contains more tiers; if the reaches are the same size, when X's failure span is
larger; if those are equal too, when X's point span is larger. Failures come
before points because tiers are defined by failures (2.5).

**The secondary statistic, always reported beside it:** the enumeration's
share of combinations that keep the scored tier (6.3), labelled as assuming
independent calls.

**Full disclosure.** Every statement comparing tier robustness names its
measure. Where the two measures order a pair of entries differently, the
statement says so. For example, on the corpus as it stands after decisions D18(b) and D26
(Session 33): by the joint readings, Ostrom-style commons governance is the
least tier-robust entry — three tiers, 7.5 points and 9 failures — and Islamic
finance the second — three tiers, 5.0 points and 9 failures, the equal failure
spans ordered by points; by the enumeration share the order of the two
reverses, since 17.1% of Islamic finance's 65,536 combinations keep its tier
against 65.6% of Ostrom's 32,768.

**Not a measure of tier robustness:** the size of the flag register. Qatar
carries twelve flags and is tier-robust; all 4,096 combinations of its calls
are Structurally Inadequate.

### 6.5 Scenarios

A scenario changes the entry's scope, boundary or another stated assumption
(3.2), not a scoring judgment within it. Scenarios are reported with their
results and never scored, and they enter neither the enumeration nor the joint
readings. Examples: Qatar with citizens only (13.0/26, 6 failures,
Structurally Inadequate); Islamic finance with its social-finance layer counted
in (14.5/26, 4 failures, Partially Adequate).

## 7. The scoring document (replaces the Appendix H.9 template)

### 7.1 Name and status line

One file per entry, `NEEC_<System>_scoring_scratch.md` (a single naming
convention for the repository is still to be settled). Under the title, a
status line says what the document is, when it was scored, and its insertion
status — stated on the current corpus, never as a dated note (10.3).

### 7.2 Sections, in order

1. **Overview.** What is scored. The scope declaration: class, archetype
   (5.1), population rule, date of the configuration where relevant, the
   extensions counted in or out and the source that decides each (3.2), and
   who confirmed the declaration. The evidentiary tier (4.1), the source mix
   (4.4), and the central disclosures.
2. **Peer calibration** (section 5): the peers declared by 5.1's rule and how
   each difference was resolved.
3. **Criterion by criterion.** Twenty-six sections in canonical order, each
   headed `#### <ID> <n>: <score> (<label>)`, where the label is Pass,
   Partial or Structural Failure, with " — flagged as contestable" appended to
   a flagged criterion. Each section quotes the Pass Threshold from
   `criteria.json` and gives the rationale with its evidence, the estimated
   performance (2.2) — for a multi-clause threshold, an estimate for each
   clause in Appendix B's order (2.3) — and the score justification; a flagged
   section also names the alternative score and what would change.
4. **Domain subtotals**, each with its addends (2.4).
5. **Summary scores:** the five domain totals, total, percent, the failures by
   criterion, and the tier.
6. **Uncertainty:** the flag register (criterion, scored value, alternatives,
   reading); the enumeration (6.3); the joint readings (6.2); tier robustness
   by the D13 measure, with the enumeration share beside it (6.4); and the
   scenarios (6.5).
7. **Where the entry sits in the corpus:** comparative claims, only as section
   8 allows; every table generated, none typed by hand.
8. **Final assessment:** what the scores mean, what would change them, and the
   evidence that will date.
9. **Reviewer disclosure:** scorer, date and session; whether a second scorer
   has cross-checked the entry; the scope decisions and who confirmed them; and
   any conflict of interest, including the self-referential-bias disclosure
   wherever the project's own framework, CCO-PTF-CIP-SZH, is scored or
   compared.
10. **The summary block** (section 9), last.

### 7.3 What a scoring document must not do

Resolve a close call silently. Score against a scope other than the one it
declares. Credit a clause it does not estimate (2.3), or a remedy from outside
its declared scope (3.2, 4.1). State a comparative claim that the corpus-level
checker does not assert. Carry dated "post-insertion" notes (10.3).

## 8. Comparative claims and the verification split (decisions D3(b), D15)

### 8.1 Two kinds of claim

- **Entry claims** are about the entry itself: its vector and arithmetic, its
  flags, joint readings and scenarios, and its own generated tables. They do
  not go stale when the corpus grows.
- **Comparative claims** are any statement of another entry's score on a
  criterion, of another entry's flags, or of the entry's position in the
  corpus: criterion bands ("alongside Georgism and ..."), pairwise
  comparisons, ranks, ties, strict dominance, domain positions and splits,
  flag counts, and tier robustness. They go stale whenever an entry is added
  or restated.

Characterisations of another entry's design, scope, evidence or archetype
("the direct analogue of Georgism's dividend") are argument rather than data,
and are not checked.

### 8.2 The split (D15)

Each entry's claims are checked by its entry verifier: `neec_entry.py` for
everything in the summary block, and an entry-specific verifier for prose
claims beyond it. **One corpus-level checker** —
`verify_comparative_claims.py`, which since the D14 pass (Session 29) covers
all eleven scoring documents — checks every comparative claim in every document, and is re-run
after every insertion or restatement. Superseded verifiers are pinned as
snapshots and keep running against the corpus they were written for.

### 8.3 Assert, don't only print (D3(b))

A comparative claim passes only if the computed fact holds **and** the sentence
stating it occurs verbatim in the document (whitespace collapsed, emphasis
markers removed). A reference to another entry's score on a criterion must sit
inside that criterion's own section. A claim that cannot be asserted is not
made.

### 8.4 Superlatives

A superlative names its reference set and its measure: "the widest ... in the
corpus, measured by ...", "the least ... among the narrow single-mechanism
entries".

## 9. The summary block (decision D3(a))

### 9.1 Placement

Every scoring document ends with exactly one summary block (the Step 1c
retrofit document, which covers thirteen systems, carries one per system),
between these markers:

````markdown
<!-- NEEC-SUMMARY-BLOCK -->
```json
{ ...the block... }
```
<!-- /NEEC-SUMMARY-BLOCK -->
````

### 9.2 Fields (schema `neec-summary-block/1.0`, `summary_block_schema.json`)

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| `schema` | `"neec-summary-block/1.0"` |
| `key` | the entry's key in the canonical corpus (for a candidate, the key it takes on insertion) |
| `code` | a short code, unique in the corpus (for example `OS`) |
| `display_name` | the entry's name in `neec_scores.csv` |
| `record` | the documents of record; when and how it was scored; `native-v2` or `retrofit-step1c` |
| `scope` | `class` (section 3); `basis` (`stated`: the entry's documents declare it; `assigned`: assigned by the project owner under decision D17; `proposed`: awaiting confirmation, used by no current entry); the `population` rule as the document states it; the `source` of the declaration |
| `vector` | the 26 scores, in canonical order |
| `summary` | the five domain totals, the total, the failure count and the tier |
| `flags` | one item per flagged criterion: `criterion`, `scored`, `alternatives`, `basis`, `reading`, `tracks`, `source` |
| `joint_readings` | one item per reading: `id`, `label`, `basis` (`scored`, `stated` or `extremes`), the flagged criteria it `resolve`s, and its `result` (total, failures, tier) |
| `scenarios` | one item per scenario: `id`, `label`, `kind` (`scope` or `other`), the criteria it `changes`, and its `result` |
| `peers` | the entries calibrated against (section 5); optional, absent for entries scored before this protocol |

### 9.3 What `neec_entry.py` checks, beyond the schema

- The entry is in the canonical corpus with exactly this vector, under its CSV
  display name.
- The summary is the arithmetic of the vector (2.4, 2.5).
- Each flag's scored value is the vector's; its alternatives are other valid
  values, in ascending order; a `both-directions` flag is a 0.5 with
  alternatives [0.0, 1.0]; a tracking flag tracks an independent flag with the
  same number of alternatives; flags appear in canonical order.
- Exactly one joint reading is the scored one; every other joint reading
  resolves only flagged criteria to their stated alternatives and moves
  tracking flags with their calls; an entry with flags and no stated readings
  carries exactly the two extremes; every stated result is recomputed.
- Every scenario changes at least one criterion to a different valid value,
  and its result is recomputed.
- Declared peers are other canonical entries.

It then reports the enumeration (6.3), the D13 measure (6.4), the undisputed
failures (failures no flag disputes), and — with `--entry CODE` — the peer
matrix (section 5). With `--corpus` it prints the corpus inventory of the D13
measure. It is deterministic and needs only the Python standard library.

**Candidate mode (`--candidate`).** For an entry not yet in the corpus — a
draft being calibrated (section 5), an entry awaiting insertion, or a
replicator's block (section 11) — the first check is reversed: the key must be
absent from the corpus, and so must the display name and the code (the
corpus's codes are read from `neec_corpus.json`); the display name begins with
the key, and the record is `native-v2`. Every other check runs as above,
except that the vector is not compared with the corpus and declared peers must
be corpus entries. The peer matrix is printed for every candidate, with its
peers' scope classes; an entry that declares no peers is told that section 5
asks for them. `--selftest --candidate` exercises these checks on a synthetic
entry. Where the canonical script is absent, as in a replication kit,
`neec_entry.py` reads the corpus from `neec_corpus.json`; beside the canonical
script, that file must match the canonical corpus exactly, or the run stops.

### 9.4 How blocks are produced

Blocks are generated, not typed. For entries scored before this protocol,
`build_summary_blocks.py` (Session 27) generated all 23 blocks and staged them
in `summary_blocks_s27.json`, taking vectors from the canonical corpus, flags
from the verifiers' registers or — for documents without one — from the prose,
with every prose-derived flag, scope statement, stated joint reading and
scenario result tied to a verbatim phrase the script asserts in its document.
Session 28 applied decisions D17 and D18(a) to the staged blocks by a separate
transform that prints every change (`stage_blocks_s28.py`, writing
`summary_blocks_s28.json`); the Session 27 file stays as generated. The Session
28 blocks were embedded in their documents in the D14 pass (Session 29,
`d14_landing_s29.py`), so that each
document is edited once. Session 33 regenerated Ostrom-style commons
governance's block when D18(b) and D26 were applied (`restate_s33.py`).

### 9.5 Layout

`neec_entry.dump_block` writes one domain per vector line and one flag, reading
or scenario per line, so that a changed score shows as a one-line difference.

## 10. Corpus maintenance

### 10.1 Scratch before insert

A scored entry stays in its scoring document until an insertion pass. No score
is spliced into the canonical files by hand.

### 10.2 Insertion

The vector and its summary are read from the entry's summary block (this
replaces the earlier practice of extracting vectors from verifier literals and
of parsing three document layouts), re-checked by `neec_entry.py` — as a
candidate before insertion (9.3) and as a canonical entry after it — and
inserted by a generator whose inputs are pinned by MD5; `neec_scores.csv` and
`neec_corpus.json` are then regenerated by their builders. Every anchored edit
must match exactly once, and the outputs are deterministic. No scoring judgment is
exercised during insertion or regeneration.

### 10.3 Restate in place (decisions D12, D14)

After an insertion, every document's comparative claims are re-tested on the
new corpus. Claims that changed are restated in the document itself, by
generator, on the new corpus — never by appending a dated note. The text as it
stood before the restatement is pinned as a snapshot (a `_sNN_snapshot` file; Git tags replace them after version 2.0).

### 10.4 Snapshots and the harness

Every data-changing pass pins its inputs and adds its scripts to
`run_all_checks.py` in the same session. The harness re-runs every script and
compares every output with its captured copy, byte for byte.

### 10.5 Generate, don't transcribe

Every table, figure and corpus position in prose is drawn from generated
output.

## 11. Replication (decision D2)

1. **Blind re-scoring.** The replicator receives a replication kit, built by
   `build_replication_kit.py` for one target: a blind copy of this protocol,
   `criteria.json` and its schema, the summary block schema, `neec_entry.py`,
   the corpus as `neec_corpus.json` with the target withheld, and a brief —
   not the original scoring document, its verifier or its block, and not the
   canonical script or CSV, which carry the target's scores. The replicator
   produces a complete scoring document with its block. The kit is delivered
   as one archive, and the harness regenerates it byte for byte.
2. **The blind copy (decision D24; revision R8).** Passages of this protocol
   that report the target's scoring — its scores, readings, flags, scope
   class, archetype, or the outcome a pilot expects — are withheld from the
   replicator's copy; no rule changes. A withheld passage is marked where it
   stood, except where the marker would itself disclose the withheld fact: an
   example withheld from a scope class's list (3.1) or from the archetype table
   (5.1) is dropped without a marker, and the note under the copy's title says
   that lists were shortened, not which (R8). The builder refuses a marker in
   3.1 or 5.1, lists every withheld passage, scans every file of the kit except
   the brief for the target's names, code and signature figures, and stops on
   any hit. The first kit was built before R8: its 3.1 marker disclosed its
   target's class and archetype (replication record, section 1), and it stays
   reproducible as issued.
3. **Validation.** The replicator checks the block with `neec_entry.py
   --candidate` against the kit's corpus (9.3). The maintainers' baseline is
   the original entry, run the same way against the same corpus.
4. **Comparison at the criterion level:** agreement of the vectors (exact
   matches, and each difference by criterion and direction); overlap of the
   flags (the same criteria flagged, with the same alternatives); agreement of
   the scope declaration; the joint readings and the D13 measure; and the tier.
5. **The replication record** names each disagreement and attributes it to
   evidence, interpretation, scope or an ambiguity in this protocol.
   Ambiguities in the protocol become revisions of it.
6. **The first pilot (revision R7).** Ostrom-style commons governance was
   re-scored blind in Session 32 by Claude Sonnet 5, in an incognito chat
   outside the Project, from the kit `build_replication_kit.py OS` builds. The
   record is `NEEC_OstromCommons_replication_record.md`; `compare_replication.py`
   computes and checks its tables. The two scorings agree on 22 of 26 criteria
   exactly, on all 26 within one step, and on the tier. Of the four
   disagreements, one was attributed to interpretation (C1.4) and three to
   this protocol, each now a rule: an anchor that contradicted itself (C3.2,
   D26), conjunctive thresholds (C4.1, D28) and replicated implementation
   failure (C4.3, D29). The scope question the pilot was built to test appeared
   not in the scored vectors but in how uncertainty was expressed, and it
   decided D18(b) (3.4). What the pilot does not show — reliability across
   scorers in general, a reproducible scope classification (the leak R8
   closes), independence from the corpus — is stated in the record, section 9.
7. **The second pilot** re-scores CCO-PTF-CIP-SZH, the entry the
   self-referential concern bears on (7.2, item 9), with a replicator outside
   the Claude family and a kit built under R8, after the rescoring pass. Its
   v1 scoring is public, so blindness cannot rest on non-publication: the
   brief forbids NEEC sources, and the record says how blindness was kept.

## 12. What the machine checks, and what it does not

**Checked:** the arithmetic; agreement with the canonical corpus (for a
candidate, absence from it); the consistency of flags, joint readings and
scenarios; the enumeration and the D13 figures; the presence and truth of
every asserted comparative sentence; every anchor example against the
published scores, and every anchor's threshold line against its definition
(`build_criteria.py`); this protocol's own statements about the corpus, its
archetype table, its decision register and Appendix B
(`verify_protocol_s36.py`); the byte-for-byte reproduction of every captured
output (`run_all_checks.py`); in a replication kit, that no file but the brief
names the target, and that no marker stands where it would disclose it
(`build_replication_kit.py`, with its R8 self-test); and the audit behind D28
(`r4_audit_s35.py`).

**Not checked:** whether the evidence supports a score — that is what flags,
peer calibration and replication are for; whether a document's clause
estimates (2.3) are present and right, which the rescoring pass and
replication examine; prose outside the asserted claims; the Report's and
Paper's prose until they are regenerated from generated tables; and the
fidelity of the Appendix G cross-validation scripts to the Compassionism
Simulation's source (on hold until the new Simulation index ships).

produced the disagreement. *Consequences.* Unlike D28, D29 can move a 0.5 to

outcomes are 0.5 as scored, or 0.0 — 14.0/26, 4 failures, Partially Adequate.

against it (131 scores, Ostrom's C4.3 among them) and reports every failure
count it changes; the rule's other cases are read where the pass meets them.

only by example (R5). *Reasons.* Peers are to be declared by rule; a table
with a test for each archetype makes that possible and checkable. The four
archetypes named in Step 1b are kept as named. One is added — comprehensive
design with committed mechanisms — because eight comprehensive-system entries
fit none of the named four. *Consequences.* No score changes. By the rule, the
first pilot would have declared six peers, the other narrow single mechanisms;

Universal Basic Income.

**D31 — extensions of a mechanism.** Raised by the replicator (R6), who met a
boundary the protocol left open. *Reasons.* The test writes down what the
corpus's four precedents did (3.2) and makes the source that decides each case
part of the document. Requiring that source, rather than the scorer's own
