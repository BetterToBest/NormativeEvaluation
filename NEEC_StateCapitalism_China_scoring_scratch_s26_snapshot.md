## 19 [pending renumbering]. State Capitalism / China

### Overview

**What is scored.** Paper Section 8.1 defines this sub-entry as a
party-state-directed market economy: extensive state-owned-enterprise (SOE)
ownership and industrial policy operating alongside private enterprise, with
markets expected to serve party-state strategic goals rather than the
reverse. This evaluation scores the People's Republic of China's post-1978
political economy as it is configured at the evaluation date (September
2026), informed by its full reform-era record. Where the reform era
(1978–2012) and the period since 2012 differ materially on a criterion, both
are described, and the score reflects the system as it currently stands.
This choice matters most for C3.4, where it is flagged.

**The system's actual shape, in numbers.** The "state capitalism" label can
mislead about proportions. A widely used summary, which Xi Jinping has
himself cited, holds that private firms contribute roughly 60% of GDP, 70%
of innovation, 80% of urban employment, and 90% of new jobs, and pay about
half of tax revenue (Harvard Kennedy School, Cunningham 2022; Seafarer
Funds). A World Bank residual estimate put the SOE share of GDP at roughly
23% for 2017. The state's reach runs less through ownership shares than
through control: party committees were formally written into corporate
articles of association in 2017, and private companies with state-connected
investors rose from about 14% of all registered capital in 2000 to about 34%
in 2019 (Bai and Hsieh, as summarized by Cunningham). Public property still
made up about 30% of national wealth in 2015 — down from about 70% in 1978,
but far above rich countries' near-zero (Piketty, Yang & Zucman, *American
Economic Review*, 2019).

**Scope boundaries, and why.**

- **The Maoist planned economy (1949–1978) is not scored here.** It falls
  within this corpus's existing Centrally Planned Socialism (CPS) entry,
  whose own C5.5 write-up already names China among the cultures where
  central planning was implemented. That makes CPS a genuine before/after
  comparator for this entry — the same country and ruling party, before and
  after reform (see Final Assessment).
- **Hong Kong and Macau are excluded.** They run under separate legal and
  economic systems, and Freedom House assesses Hong Kong separately. The
  dismantling of "one country, two systems" after 2020 is used only as
  evidence under C5.3.
- **Singapore and the Gulf sovereign-wealth-fund states are reserved** for
  their own sub-entries, exactly as Section 8.1 specifies.

**A contested label, used here as taxonomy only.** Beijing describes the
system as a "socialist market economy." A free-market critique argues that
China's growth record belongs to its private sector rather than to the
state, and that "state capitalism" is therefore a misleading term
(Zitelmann, *Forbes*, 2019). Left critics describe the system as capitalism
under party-state management (see C4.5). Section 8.1's "state capitalism" is
used here as this corpus's taxonomic term, not as an endorsement of any of
these framings.

**The normative-commitment disclosure this entry especially needs.** NEEC is
explicitly normative (Paper Section 2.2). It embeds commitments to freedom
from coercion, democratic participation, and diffused power that any
one-party system fails by construction. Several scores below (C2.1, C2.4,
C4.4) therefore follow from the framework's stated values as much as from
contested facts. Section 2.2 itself invites readers who reject those
commitments to contest them at the level of the criteria, through Appendix
I, rather than through individual scores. This entry takes the framework as
specified.

**Evidentiary tier (Appendix H.6): real-world implementation, with an
explicit reliability discount.** Like Status Quo Market Capitalism and
Nordic Social Democracy, this system is scored from documented outcomes. But
several official data series relevant here are contested, suspended, or
unavailable at the microdata level. The World Bank's own China poverty and
inequality estimates rely on grouped consumption data that the National
Bureau of Statistics (NBS) last shared for 2019. The youth-unemployment
series was suspended in 2023 and resumed on a narrower definition. Official
COVID-19 exit-wave mortality is a small fraction of independent estimates.
Local statistical concealment is documented by the State Council's own
investigations. (All are discussed under C3.5.) Where official and
independent figures diverge, both are reported, and the score rests on the
independent estimate unless stated otherwise.

**Where this system sits relative to the corpus.** It is the first Step 1b
system that is a whole existing national political economy rather than a
reform proposal or a single mechanism, so it fits none of the three
structural archetypes named during Step 1b (see Final Assessment). In kind,
it belongs with the Report's Systems 1–5, the "existing major frameworks."

### Domain 1: Material Security

#### C1.1 Poverty Elimination Capacity: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** At the extreme-poverty line, China's record is historically
unprecedented. The joint study by the World Bank and the State Council's
Development Research Center (2022) finds that the number of people in China
below $1.90 a day fell by close to 800 million over four decades — nearly
three-quarters of the global reduction — and by 770 million at China's
national line. In 2021 China declared rural poverty eliminated at its
official standard, which the World Bank equates to about $2.30 a day in 2017
purchasing-power terms.

The question for NEEC is which line applies. Appendix C.1's Poverty
Measurement Protocol is explicit: low-income countries use the World Bank's
absolute line, while middle-income countries adjust the basket to local
norms. China has been an upper-middle-income country since 2010, and the
World Bank's own briefs describe $6.85 a day (2017 PPP) as the line typical
of upper-middle-income economies such as China. On that line, per the World
Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform, China's poverty rate peaked at 99%
in 1990 and stood at 24.8% in 2020 and 17.0% in 2021; the World Bank's April
2023 brief put 2019 at 24.7%, with more than a third of that group living in
cities. That is a reduction of about 75% over the thirty years from 1990 to
2020. The intermediate annual values needed for an exact twenty-year figure
were not retrieved. Bracketed by the citable endpoints, the continuous
estimate for a twenty-year window ending in 2020–2021 is roughly 70–80% —
inside the Partial band (roughly 30–89%).

Two features of the mechanism reinforce the Partial reading. First, the
income floor is narrow. China's minimum-living-guarantee program (dibao)
covered 40.17 million people in 2022, under 3% of the population, after a
decade of continuous decline from a peak near 5% (UNICEF budget brief).
World Bank research on rural dibao (2007–2009 data) found it gives
recipients adequate income but does little to reduce overall poverty,
because recipients are few relative to the poor and targeting errors are
large (Golan, Sicular & Umapathi, 2014). Second, old-age provision is
two-tiered: the resident pension that covers rural residents and informal
workers averages about 179 yuan a month, under $25 (Benelux Chamber of
Commerce in Shanghai, citing official data) — far below any
upper-middle-income poverty line.

One further point is disclosed here because it concerns the same apparatus,
though it is scored elsewhere. UN human rights experts reported in January
2026 that the state-mandated "poverty alleviation through labour transfer"
programme enables forced labour of Uyghur, Tibetan, and other minority
workers (see C2.1, C4.3, C4.5). Part of the machinery credited with poverty
reduction is therefore also coercion evidence.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, large, well-documented
reduction, but about 70–80% at the line Appendix C.1 prescribes for a
middle-income country, short of the 90% Pass Threshold. **Flagged as
contestable:** measured at the extreme or national line, the reduction
exceeds 99%, and this corpus's own CPS entry scored 1.0 on a similar
"near-universal elimination of absolute poverty" basis. This evaluation
follows the protocol's explicit middle-income rule instead — a rule that
would apply equally to any upper-middle-income system, not a China-specific
stringency. Consequence, checked: resolving to 1.0 raises the total to
10.5/26 and leaves the failure count and tier unchanged.

#### C1.2a Wealth Building for Resilience: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Unlike every other Step 1b system except Sovereign Wealth
Fund Statism, China has a genuine, individually titled accumulation
mechanism that reaches most households: owner-occupied housing, backed by
high household saving. Home ownership stood at about 90% in Peking
University's Institute of Social Science survey (89.7% in 2018). UBS's
*Global Wealth Report 2023* put median wealth per adult at $27,273 and mean
wealth at $75,731 (end-2022), and attributed much of the fivefold rise in
global median wealth this century to China. Section 12.3's
cultural-adaptation clause scales the $70,000 buffer for middle-income
countries to 150–200% of national median annual household expenses. This
evaluation's own rough estimate — reasoning, not a citable figure — is that
China's median household likely clears that adjusted bar on magnitude alone.

The criterion's purpose, though, is resilience, and on current evidence the
buffer's resilience is materially impaired:

- **Concentration in one falling asset.** Housing makes up close to 70% of
  household wealth (Stanford Center on China's Economy and Institutions,
  2026). New-home prices across 70 cities fell for thirty consecutive months
  through December 2025 and were still falling year-on-year in February 2026
  (NBS data via Trading Economics). Stanford's analysts put cumulative
  declines at about 20% officially and up to 40% unofficially. UBS recorded
  a 2.2% fall in wealth per adult in 2022, with total assets per adult
  falling for the first time since 2000.
- **Limited diversification and shock absorption.** Stanford's analysis
  notes that capital controls leave households few alternatives to property,
  and that China lacks the safety net that would let households smooth
  income shocks, so housing losses hit consumption unusually hard.
- **Rural assets that cannot serve as a buffer.** Rural residents hold free,
  indefinite use rights to homestead land but historically could not sell,
  mortgage, or lease them (*Journal of Rural Studies*, 2026), and homestead
  transfer remains severely restricted (*Land Use Policy*, 2018). The
  president of the Beijing-based Center for China and Globalization has
  argued publicly that easing these restrictions is needed for rural
  residents to build wealth.
- **Access gates in the largest cities.** Without local household
  registration (hukou), buying a home in Shanghai is described as almost
  impossible (CKGSB, 2023).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, broad, individually held
mechanism whose magnitude likely clears the protocol's
middle-income-adjusted bar, but whose value as a resilience buffer is
impaired by concentration in an asset class now in its fifth year of
decline, restricted rural transferability, and capital controls. The
continuous estimate (about 0.6–0.75) straddles the rounding boundary and is
disclosed as such, per H.3. **Flagged as contestable:** a magnitude-first
reading of the adjusted threshold would score 1.0. Consequence, checked:
10.5/26, failure count and tier unchanged. C1.5 would stay at 0.5 on its
own, independent gating evidence.

#### C1.2b Prevention of Exploitative Accumulation: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS estimates China's wealth Gini at 70.9 (end-2022). That is
inside the "social democracy: Gini 0.65–0.75" band that Section 12.3 already
uses, and well below North America's 82.3 in the same dataset. The trend,
however, runs the wrong way. Piketty, Yang & Zucman find that the top 10%
income share rose from 27% to 41% between 1978 and 2015 while the bottom 50%
share fell from 27% to 15% — a move from near-Nordic toward US levels — and
that the top 10% wealth share reached about 67%, close to the US's 72% (as
summarized by Stanford SCCEI).

Two structural features do limit private concentration in specific channels:
public property's roughly 30% share of national wealth, and collective
ownership of rural land, which capital cannot simply buy up. The party-state
has also shown it will discipline private wealth that looks politically
threatening. China Leadership Monitor's analysis of the 2021 "common
prosperity" push found it heavy on crackdowns on capital while actual
redistribution was extremely limited or superficial. Against these sits a
privilege channel of the kind this corpus's CPS entry weighed: civil
servants' pensions, estimated at 6,000–7,000 yuan a month (MERICS, 2025, via
*Christian Science Monitor*), against the roughly 179-yuan average in the
resident scheme.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — real structural limits on some
concentration channels and a wealth Gini in the social-democratic band, but
a documented upward trend toward US levels and a state-linked privilege
channel. This matches both corpus precedents it most resembles: Nordic
Social Democracy's 0.5 (same Gini band) and CPS's 0.5 (formal compression
alongside de facto privilege).

#### C1.3 Housing Security: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** China has run state housing mechanisms at a scale no other
system in this corpus can point to. Under shantytown redevelopment, the
government invested 1.48 trillion yuan in 2016 alone to build 6.06 million
homes, within an 18-million-home, three-year program and a wider goal of
rehousing about 100 million people living in shantytowns and urban villages
(State Council, as reported by Xinhua and SCIO, 2017). In May 2024, Beijing
allowed local governments to buy unsold apartments and pledged to complete
unfinished homes (AFP). Ownership tenure is broad and lightly leveraged:
only about 18% of homeowners had a mortgage in 2022 (*Foreign Policy*).

The market side is where stability breaks down:

- **Pre-sale failure.** By 2022, more than 85% of homes were sold before
  completion (*Foreign Policy*). That summer, mortgage boycotts over stalled
  projects spread to more than 300 developments, and analysts' estimates of
  unfinished projects ranged from 5% to 20% nationally (Reuters, via *Daily
  Sabah*). Officials censored social-media posts about the boycotts
  (*Marketplace*). Freedom House's China Dissent Monitor counted 1,841
  property-related protests between June 2022 and September 2023, about a
  quarter of which met police repression (VOA).
- **Forced relocation within the state mechanism itself.** A peer-reviewed
  study of shantytown redevelopment reports that about 12.6 million
  households were moved as their dwellings were demolished between 2008 and
  2012, and that the 1998 suspension of public housing provision left
  low-income residents without subsidy eligibility with very limited access
  to housing (*Cities*, 2017).
- **Hukou exclusion.** China's roughly 300 million migrant workers, over 40%
  of the labour force (BOFIT, 2026), have historically been shut out of city
  housing support and many public services where they live and work.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — massive, real state intervention
alongside market allocation marked by documented pre-sale failure, forced
relocation, and a large excluded migrant population. This sits with Market
Socialism's and UBI's 0.5 pattern and below CPS's 1.0, which rested on
universal state allocation.

#### C1.4 Automation Resilience: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** China is the most automation-exposed large economy in this
corpus. It accounted for 54% of global industrial-robot installations in
2024 (295,000 units), and its operational robot stock passed 2 million after
doubling in three years (International Federation of Robotics, *World
Robotics 2025*). The household-side buffer is thin: dibao reaches under 3%
of the population (C1.1). Urban youth unemployment hit a record 21.3% in
June 2023; publication was then suspended, resumed on a definition that
excludes students, and reached 18.9% under the new definition in August 2025
before easing to 16.1% in February 2026 (NBS, as reported).

A real, if partial, mechanism exists that most market systems lack: the
state can direct large employers to absorb workers. In 2023, Anhui and
Guizhou required at least half of SOE new hires to be recent graduates
(SCMP), and Hunan added graduate recruitment to state-firm executives'
performance appraisals (WION). A May 2026 national employment plan requires
SOEs to raise campus recruitment by more than five percentage points and —
notably for this criterion — calls for retraining workers affected by AI
adoption (China Society for Human Rights Studies, state-affiliated). In June
2026, eight government departments launched a nationwide campaign requiring
SOEs to hire more graduates (SCMP). The state can also sustain aggregate
demand through directed investment (C3.1). None of this decouples household
income from employment, and no evidence was located that it would hold at
NEEC's 50–70% displacement severities; retraining presumes jobs to retrain
into.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a genuine, repeatedly used,
state-directed employment buffer with an explicit (if nascent)
AI-displacement component, but no decoupling of income from employment. This
matches Nordic's and CPS's 0.5 and sits above Status Quo's 0.0, which rests
on the absence of any such lever.

#### C1.5 Universal Wealth Access (narrowed — access breadth only): 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Measured by headcount, access looks broad (about 90% home
ownership). Measured the way this criterion asks — an active pathway
producing asset growth, not gated by membership or status — it is gated by
hukou. Rural hukou holders' principal asset is a restricted use right
(C1.2a); migrants face purchase restrictions in the largest cities; and the
IMF (February 2026) describes hukou as effectively excluding rural migrants
from full access to social benefits in large cities. That is the
gated-access pattern H.7v2's Market Socialism anchor describes.

Reform is under way, and it is disclosed because it bears directly on this
score's trajectory. A May 2026 policy lets workers enrol in social insurance
where they work, regardless of hukou (SCMP), and new guidance directs cities
to open schooling, social security, health care, and rental housing to
migrants — though, per BOFIT, it sets no reform schedule and leaves the pace
to local governments.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — broad nominal access, with a
meaningful population segment gated out by registration status. Of the
Domain 1 criteria, this is the most likely to move upward if hukou reform
and rural land-rights liberalization are completed.

**Domain 1 = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 3.0/6 (50%)**

### Domain 2: Human Autonomy

#### C2.1 Freedom from Coercion: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** This band's anchor case is CPS, which eliminated market
coercion but replaced it with state coercion. China's system does not make
even that trade: it keeps market coercion and adds state coercion.

- **Economic compulsion.** Survival rests on employment, with only a narrow
  means-tested floor (C1.1, C2.2). Enterprise employees worked about 48.7
  hours a week on average in 2024 (NBS, via Statista), against a statutory
  44-hour week. China's top court and labour ministry declared the "996"
  schedule (9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week) illegal in 2021, but
  enforcement stayed sporadic until an intensified push in 2025 (*Business
  Standard*; Harris Sliwoski).
- **Political and administrative coercion.** Freedom House's 2026 report
  describes a repressive authoritarian regime that keeps tight control over
  the bureaucracy, media, online speech, religious practice, universities,
  and businesses. Workers cannot form unions outside the party-affiliated
  All-China Federation of Trade Unions (US Congressional-Executive
  Commission on China).
- **Coerced labour.** UN human rights experts reported in January 2026 that
  the state-mandated labour-transfer programme places Uyghur, Tibetan, and
  other minority workers into jobs under surveillance, with no real choice
  to refuse; Xinjiang's 2021–2025 plan projected 13.75 million instances of
  labour transfer. China rejects forced-labour allegations as untrue and
  politically motivated (Reuters, 2022).

Appendix C.2's survey protocol, including its adjustment for collectivist
cultures, does not change this call, because the evidence here is structural
and legal rather than survey-based.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — two forms of coercion
coexisting, neither eliminated, with state-organized coerced labour
documented by UN experts. On the anchor's own logic, this sits below Status
Quo's 0.5, which rests on economic compulsion alone.

#### C2.2 Labor Non-Necessity: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** The system's leadership rejects this criterion's premise
explicitly. Xi Jinping's August 2021 speech on common prosperity, published
in *Qiushi*, warns against setting excessively high goals or providing
excessive guarantees, and against falling into a "welfarism" trap that
encourages laziness (Caixin full text). His December 2021 remarks to the
Central Economic Work Conference repeat the point, citing Latin American
welfare states as the cautionary example (Ginger River translation). In
practice, the only floor that resembles unconditional provision, dibao, is
means-tested and shrinking — under 3% coverage in 2022 (C1.1). Pension
eligibility is also tightening: the minimum contribution period rises from
15 to 20 years starting in 2030 (State Council Information Office).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — no baseline independent
of labour-market participation, and an explicit design-level commitment
against one. This matches both corpus anchors for this band: Status Quo (no
unconditional baseline) and MMT + Job Guarantee (ideological rejection of
labour non-necessity).

#### C2.3 Creative Development Opportunities: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Two constraints operate at once.

- **Time.** Average enterprise working hours of about 48.7 a week exceed
  both the legal norm and the Status Quo baseline, which this corpus already
  scores 0.5 for time scarcity.
- **Content control.** In 2021 the broadcast regulator ordered an end to
  effeminate men and other "abnormal" aesthetics on television and told
  broadcasters to promote revolutionary and advanced socialist culture. The
  same week, minors' online gaming was capped at three hours a week, and new
  game titles already needed government approval before release (NPR).
  Freedom House describes party control extending over media, online speech,
  and universities.

The evidence does not support a reading of wholesale suppression. China's
cultural economy is large, commercial, and globally competitive — Tencent's
*Honor of Kings* is popular worldwide (CBS News) — and most everyday
creative participation is non-political. Some parents also welcomed the
gaming limits (CBS News).

The difference from the CPS precedent (0.0) is one of kind as well as
degree. CPS's failure rested on state control of cultural production, with
culture subordinated to ideology and no private cultural market. China's
cultural sector is predominantly market-based and operates under
pre-approval and content directives that bound *what* is made rather than
*whether* people make it. H.7's 0.0 anchor speaks of structural suppression
of creative activity; its 0.5 anchor speaks of engagement meaningfully
constrained. The second fits better.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — widespread creative engagement,
meaningfully constrained by both time scarcity and structural content
control. The continuous estimate (about 0.2–0.5) sits near the rounding
boundary. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader applying the CPS precedent to
China's documented content regime would score 0.0. Consequence, checked:
9.5/26 with 9 failures, tier unchanged.

#### C2.4 Democratic Participation: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** China's own account deserves to be stated first. The State
Council's 2021 white paper *China: Democracy That Works* describes
"whole-process people's democracy": deputies to township and county people's
congresses are directly elected, with participation around 90% and more than
900 million voters in the 2016–2017 round, and legislative outreach offices
had gathered about 7,800 suggestions on 127 draft laws.

The structure around those elections is what this criterion tests. Deputies
above the county level are chosen indirectly by the congress one level below
(the same white paper). The 2018 constitutional amendment abolishing
presidential term limits passed 2,958 to 2, with 3 abstentions
(Congressional Research Service). Freedom House reports that a multiyear
crackdown has devastated independent civil society, NGOs, and human rights
defenders. Independent unions are prohibited (C2.1). And high measured
satisfaction with the central government is subject to documented preference
falsification (C5.4).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — single-party rule with
no competitive path to changing national leadership or policy. This matches
the CPS anchor for this band: single-party rule eliminating meaningful
democratic participation.

#### C2.5 Exit Rights and Mobility: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Most citizens can legally travel and emigrate. The
restrictions that do exist are imposed by the system itself rather than by
incidental economic cost:

- **Internal mobility with differential treatment.** Hukou ties schooling,
  health care, and social insurance to registration rather than residence
  for a migrant workforce of about 300 million (BOFIT; IMF). That directly
  contradicts the threshold's "no differential treatment" clause.
- **Exit bans.** Safeguard Defenders counted 15 laws authorizing exit bans
  and estimated that tens of thousands of people are under one at any time.
  A Reuters analysis of Supreme People's Court records found an eight-fold
  rise in cases mentioning exit bans between 2016 and 2022, most in civil
  rather than criminal matters (NBC News/Reuters, 2023). New exit-entry
  provisions issued in 2026 set out further grounds for barring citizens
  from leaving (DLA Piper; France 24).
- **Blanket restriction during zero-COVID.** In May 2022 the National
  Immigration Administration announced it would strictly restrict
  non-essential departures (Radio Free Asia; *Fortune*).

The current direction is mixed: 2026 reforms loosen the link between hukou
and services (C1.5), while exit controls tighten.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — formal exit exists but is
constrained by system-imposed differential treatment and expanding exit
bans. A consistency check against the corpus: CPS scored 0.5 on harsher
facts (the propiska system plus near-prohibited emigration), so this
evidence cannot support a score below CPS's. It cannot support 1.0 either,
because hukou fails the threshold's non-differential-treatment clause
outright.

**Domain 2 = 0.0 + 0.0 + 0.5 + 0.0 + 0.5 = 1.0/5 (20%)**

### Domain 3: System Resilience

#### C3.1 Crisis Response Capacity: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Speed without legislative delay is a genuine strength. The
State Council decided on its 4-trillion-yuan stimulus on 5 November 2008 and
announced it on 9 November. The package equalled about 12.5% of 2008 GDP and
was spent over roughly 27 months, and China's GDP grew more than 9% in both
2008 and 2009 (Naughton, *China Leadership Monitor*; Council on Foreign
Relations; World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6221). A US–Hong Kong
modeling study found that during 2020 and 2021 mainland China had among the
lowest estimated excess mortality of the 75 countries compared (2023, as
reported by CIDRAP).

It is not, however, the Pass Threshold's automatic, household-level, 72-hour
trigger. The 2008 package was infrastructure-heavy, and critics on the right
argued that much of it repackaged spending already announced (Heritage
Foundation). And early information about the COVID-19 outbreak was
suppressed for weeks (C3.5), the opposite of an automatic trigger.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — fast, large, discretionary state
mobilization without legislative delay, but neither automatic nor
household-level. This matches CPS's 0.5 (rapid resource mobilization in
crises) and sits above Status Quo's 0.0, which rests on months of
legislative delay.

#### C3.2 Inflation Control Mechanisms: 1.0 (Pass) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** China passed this threshold's stress test in the one recent
real-world instance of it. Consumer prices rose 2.0% in 2022, below the
government's 3% target, while inflation ran around 8% in the United States,
above 8% in the eurozone, and around 9% in the UK (National Development and
Reform Commission, via SCIO). The mechanisms are specific and named. In
September 2022 the state released about 200,000 tons of pork from government
reserves, its largest monthly release on record, and it runs
price-management schemes intended to keep pork and coal prices within set
ranges (gov.cn), alongside conventional monetary policy. Consumer inflation
peaked at 2.8% that September (Statista), and long-run inflation has stayed
within the threshold's 3% bar.

Two caveats are disclosed. First, the favourable 2022 result is partly the
government's own attribution; lockdown-suppressed demand probably
contributed. Second, the problem has since inverted. The IMF's 2025 Article
IV consultation reports core inflation averaging only 0.5% in 2024, headline
inflation hovering around zero, and elevated debt-deflation risk; headline
inflation was 0.8% in August 2026 (Trading Economics).

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — specific, named, demonstrated
anti-inflation mechanisms that met the stress-test bar in 2022. This is
consistent with both corpus precedents for existing systems: Status Quo's
1.0 for conventional monetary policy and CPS's 1.0 for administered prices.
**Flagged as contestable:** the threshold as written tests inflation only,
and a reader treating price stability symmetrically would count the
deflation record and score 0.5. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26, failure count
and tier unchanged. This evaluation weighs the deflation record under C3.3
instead.

#### C3.3 Multi-Failure Resistance: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The system has absorbed large shocks without collapse. Growth
stayed above 9% through the 2008–09 global crisis, recovered to 8.4% in
2021, and fell to 3.0% in 2022 under zero-COVID lockdowns (World Bank).
State-owned banks and capital controls give it tools to roll over and absorb
losses that market systems lack, and Stanford SCCEI's analysis argues that
the property slump can curb growth for years even without a banking crisis.

It now faces a live compound-stress test:

- **Property:** prices falling for more than two years (C1.2a).
- **Local-government debt:** the IMF estimates debt in local-government
  financing vehicles at about 58–60 trillion yuan, roughly 48% of GDP,
  against the Finance Ministry's figure of 14.3 trillion yuan for more
  narrowly defined "hidden debt" (Radio Free Asia; Associated Press).
  IMF-based augmented estimates put total public debt near 124–125% of GDP
  at end-2023 (as reported by IREF Europe and Deluair). The IMF's
  financial-system assessment calls this debt a serious risk to financial
  stability — a finding China's executive director at the IMF rejected,
  calling the problem resolved (Atlantic Council, 2026).
- **Deflation and weak demand:** see C3.2 and C4.5.
- **Demographics:** China recorded its lowest birth rate on record in 2025
  (CKGSB).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — demonstrated shock absorption
through state control of finance, but no design for compound shocks, and
risk concentrated in precisely the channels now under simultaneous stress.
Not 0.0: unlike CPS, this system has not collapsed under compound stress.

#### C3.4 Epistemic Adaptability: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** The reform era is the development literature's leading
example of evidence-based adaptation. Heilmann's "experimentation under
hierarchy" describes local experiments that were legitimized and, if they
worked, scaled into national policy, a process he credits with redefining
basic policy parameters (Harvard Center for International Development,
Working Paper 172, 2008). Vietnam later emulated the model (C5.1).

Since 2012 the picture is contested. Under "top-level design," many pilot
programs have been placed under a central leading group headed by Xi (Tokyo
Foundation). The mainstream academic view holds that local experimentation
has declined, but one study of ten cities' regulations from 2010 to 2019
found a growing share authorizing or building on local experiments (as
summarized by *The Asia Cable*).

The threshold's own terms expose the weak points:

- **Democratic governance for changes:** absent by design.
- **Timely updates without collapse:** zero-COVID was maintained for almost
  three years and then largely abandoned on 7 December 2022. China
  Leadership Monitor calls this the most dramatic reversal of one of Xi's
  signature policies, made apparently without much preparation for the exit
  wave. Independent estimates of the resulting deaths range from 1.41
  million (the US–Hong Kong modeling study cited under C3.1) to 1.87 million
  (*JAMA Network Open*, 2023; 95% interval 0.71–4.43 million, adults 30 and
  over), against about 60,000 officially reported hospital deaths. A 2026
  study of elite death notices (*Journal of Population Economics*,
  summarized by Stanford SCCEI) puts a lower bound of 1.44 million excess
  deaths among people over 65.
- **Evidence availability:** suspending a data series when it worsens (C3.5)
  weakens the evidence base that adaptation depends on.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a genuine and widely studied
adaptive capacity, now weakened by centralization, and shown to fail badly
in the largest recent adjustment. **Flagged as contestable:** scoring the
reform era alone would support 1.0, as Market Socialism's documented record
does. This evaluation scores the system as currently configured (see
Overview). Consequence, checked: 10.5/26, tier unchanged.

#### C3.5 Failure-Mode Transparency: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable; explicit divergence from the CPS precedent

**Rationale:** Active suppression of failure information is documented
across several domains, often by the Chinese state's own later
investigations:

- **Epidemic early warning.** On 30 December 2019, Dr. Li Wenliang warned
  colleagues about SARS-like cases in Wuhan; the city health commission said
  the next day that no clear human-to-human transmission had been found, and
  on 3 January 2020 police reprimanded him for spreading false information
  online. The National Supervisory Commission later found the reprimand
  inappropriate, and police revoked it (*China Daily*, March 2020). The
  Chinese government disputes that the episode shows an intent to suppress
  outbreak information (PBS *Frontline*).
- **Disaster casualties.** The State Council's investigation of the July
  2021 Henan floods found that Zhengzhou officials "deliberately impeded and
  withheld reports of up to 139 cases" of deaths and missing people (CNN,
  quoting Xinhua). The toll was revised to 398 dead or missing; 89 officials
  were disciplined and eight people were detained (CNN; *People's Daily*).
  During the disaster, a censorship directive steered domestic coverage and
  foreign journalists were harassed (China Digital Times).
- **Economic statistics.** Youth-unemployment publication was suspended
  after a record 21.3% reading and resumed on a new, student-excluding
  methodology whose first published figure was more than six points lower —
  a gap that partly reflects seasonal patterns as well as the definition
  (*The Business Standard*, 2025; *Time*). The World Bank's own poverty
  estimates rely on grouped data last shared for 2019 (Overview). Official
  and IMF measures of local-government debt differ roughly fourfold, partly
  by definition (C3.3).
- **Mortality.** Officially reported exit-wave COVID-19 deaths were a small
  fraction of every independent estimate (C3.4).
- **Protest and labour information.** Officials censored posts about
  mortgage boycotts (C1.3). China Labour Bulletin, which had logged 1,789
  worker protests in 2023 and 1,508 in 2024, dissolved in June 2025, and its
  website went offline almost immediately (China Unofficial Archives).

The genuine counterweight is that central investigations do surface and
punish concealment, as the Henan and Li Wenliang cases show. Those
corrections were episodic and took months — far outside the threshold's
one-week detection bar.

**Why this diverges from the CPS precedent.** CPS scored 1.0 because its
failures — shortages and queues — were highly visible and legible to the
population even while politically suppressed. That reasoning does not
transfer. A shortage economy's signature failure cannot be hidden from
consumers. China's signature failure modes — debt accumulation, epidemic
onset, disaster tolls, statistical series — are exactly the kind that can be
hidden, and are documented as having been. H.7's own 0.0 anchor names active
suppression of failure information.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — systematic, multi-domain
suppression, with a real but slow and episodic central-correction channel.
**Flagged as contestable:** a reader applying the CPS precedent, or
weighting the central-correction mechanism more heavily, would score 0.5.
Consequence, checked: 10.5/26 with 7 failures, still Structurally Inadequate
on its own (see Summary Scores for the joint case).

**Domain 3 = 0.5 + 1.0 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.0 = 2.5/5 (50%)**

### Domain 4: Ethical Integrity

#### C4.1 Intergenerational Justice: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The positive transfers are real and large. China's
clean-energy buildout is the world's largest: in the first nine months of
2025 it completed 240 GW of solar and 61 GW of wind capacity, and it added a
record 75 GW of energy storage over the year, while its CO2 emissions have
been flat or falling since March 2024 (Carbon Brief analyses by the Centre
for Research on Energy and Clean Air, CREA). That buildout has plausibly
lowered clean-technology costs for other countries as well — a reasoned
point this evaluation does not quantify.

The negative transfers are also large. China remains the world's largest
emitter (AFP). Public debt runs well above the threshold's 80%-of-GDP bar on
IMF-based augmented measures (C3.3). The pension system is under visible
strain: the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences projected in 2019 that the
urban employee pension fund could be exhausted by 2035 (MERICS), and the
statutory retirement age began rising in January 2025, the first increase in
decades (State Council Information Office). The demographic legacy of the
one-child policy now shows in a record-low birth rate (C3.3).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — substantial structural positive
transfer coexisting with significant negative transfer, the pattern
described by this band's Nordic anchor. Not 0.0, because genuine structural
counterweights exist.

#### C4.2 Ecological Compliance: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** H.7 instructs scorers to hold this criterion to
absolute-reduction standards. On that basis:

- **Operative targets have been relative, and were missed.** The 2021–2025
  target was an 18% cut in carbon intensity; the achieved figure was about
  12%, and meeting China's Paris intensity commitment now requires roughly a
  23% cut over the next five years (CREA, for Carbon Brief).
- **The first economy-wide absolute target is modest and open-ended.**
  Announced in September 2025, it pledges a 7–10% cut below *peak* emissions
  by 2035, with the peak year left undefined. Carbon Brief notes that a
  peak-based target can reward provinces for raising emissions before the
  peak — "storming the peak."
- **Coal is still expanding.** More than 200 GW of coal power was approved
  in 2022–2023 — more than the entire US coal fleet — and China commissioned
  70% of the world's new coal capacity in 2024 (Global Energy Monitor). In
  2025, new and reactivated coal proposals reached a record 161 GW, 78 GW
  was commissioned, and 291 GW remained permitted or under construction,
  about 23% of the operating fleet (CREA and GEM, February 2026). CREA reads
  the proposal surge partly as a push to advance projects before the 2030
  peaking deadline.

The counter-evidence is significant, and it is why this call is flagged.
Renewables are now covering nearly all new electricity demand; coal
generation fell 1.9% in 2025; and 2025 emissions likely dipped 0.3%,
although CREA's lead analyst cautions the drop is too small to call a
definite fall (AFP).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — growth and
energy-security imperatives not subordinated to ecological limits, relative
targets substituting for absolute ones through 2025, and coal capacity still
expanding. No current pledge is on a path toward the 35–45% absolute cut the
Pass Threshold requires by 2030. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader
weighting the emissions plateau and the renewable buildout more heavily
would apply the Nordic anchor for 0.5 (leading in renewable energy, but
still exceeding planetary boundaries). Consequence, checked: 10.5/26 with 7
failures, tier unchanged on its own. For consistency, note that Status Quo's
own 0.0 rests on a structural growth-imperative argument rather than on the
recent direction of emissions, and the same reasoning is applied here. This
is the criterion most likely to move with time: the Party's leadership has
said a "dual control" system covering total carbon emissions should take
effect during the 15th Five-Year Plan period (2026–2030) (Carbon Brief).

#### C4.3 Racial and Gender Equity: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable; explicit divergence from the CPS precedent

**Rationale — ethnic equity.** In August 2022 the UN human rights office
concluded that the extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention of
Uyghurs and other predominantly Muslim groups in Xinjiang "may constitute
international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity" (UN News). It
reported credible allegations of torture, sexual violence, forced medical
treatment, and coercive family planning (*Eurasia Review*; Amnesty
International). China rejects these findings, citing its obligations to
combat terrorism (*Michigan Journal of International Law*), and an October
2022 Human Rights Council motion merely to debate the report was defeated
(*Eurasia Review*). In 2022 the ILO's Committee of Experts expressed "deep
concern" about discriminatory policy in Xinjiang and asked China to repeal
provisions imposing de-radicalisation duties on enterprises and trade unions
(Reuters); China ratified the ILO's two forced-labour conventions that
August (ILO). UN experts reported in January 2026 that coercive labour
transfers of minority workers may amount to forcible transfer or enslavement
as crimes against humanity. In March 2026 the National People's Congress
adopted a Law on Promoting Ethnic Unity and Progress, which NPC Observer
describes as codifying an assimilationist ethnic doctrine.

China also has a genuine history of affirmative policy. Minority students
have received bonus points on the university entrance exam — 10 to 20 points
in Guizhou, where the policy drew protests from Han parents in 2018 (*Global
Times*) — and ethnic minorities were exempted from the one-child limit.
These are real disproportionate-benefit mechanisms of the kind H.7's 1.0
anchor describes.

**Rationale — gender equity.** The Politburo chosen in 2022 has no women,
breaking a two-decade norm of at least one; no woman has ever sat on the
Politburo Standing Committee or the Central Military Commission; and 13 of
the Central Committee's 203 members were women as of August 2025 (CSIS
ChinaPower). China ranks 146th of 148 countries on sex ratio at birth in the
World Economic Forum's 2025 report (same source). Women's share of party
membership has risen to 31%, up from 19% in 2004 (same source). Female
labour-force participation was among the world's highest in 1990, but the
gap with men has widened since (*Forbes*). Feminist activists have been
prosecuted: #MeToo journalist Huang Xueqin was sentenced to five years in
June 2024 (HEAD Foundation).

**Why this diverges from the CPS precedent.** CPS scored 1.0 on a strong
ideological commitment to equality. China's leadership likewise asserts that
all ethnic groups have achieved genuine equality under socialism (Xi
Jinping, as reported by *Tracking People's Daily*). But H.7's anchors ask
for measured disproportionate benefit and convergence, and the current
documented record for the minorities most affected runs the other way. The
continuous estimate weighs real affirmative mechanisms against documented
state coercion targeting specific minorities — a harm more severe than the
"formally neutral policy" that H.7's 0.0 anchor describes — together with
declining top-level gender representation. It lands at or below 0.25.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0). **Flagged as
contestable:** weighting the preferential policies more heavily gives 0.5
(10.5/26, 7 failures), and applying the CPS precedent's own reasoning gives
1.0 (11.0/26, 7 failures). Neither changes the tier on its own.

#### C4.4 Power Distribution: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Both forms of power are concentrated. Politically, the 2018
amendment removed presidential term limits by 2,958 votes to 2
(Congressional Research Service); Xi Jinping secured a third term as party
leader in 2022, with a Politburo Standing Committee made up of his allies
(NBC News); and Freedom House describes personal power consolidated to a
degree not seen in decades. Economically, the top 10% hold about two-thirds
of wealth (C1.2b).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — concentration in a
narrow group, matching both the CPS anchor's bureaucratic pattern and the
Status Quo anchor's wealth pattern.

#### C4.5 Exploitation Elimination: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** The CPS precedent (1.0) credited the abolition of private
capital; it does not transfer to a system in which private firms provide
about 80% of urban employment. Three lines of evidence establish extraction
as structural here:

- **State-organized coerced labour** (C2.1, C4.3). The US Department of
  Labor identified ten products made with forced labour in Xinjiang,
  including cotton, textiles, tomatoes, hair products, and polysilicon
  (2022).
- **No independent counterweight in the capital–labour relationship.**
  Independent unions are prohibited (C2.1). Critics on the left argue that
  the official federation has never backed a workers' protest and has taken
  part in strike suppression (China Worker). China Labour Bulletin still
  logged 1,508 worker protests in 2024 before its closure (C3.5).
- **Two-tier labour citizenship** through hukou (C1.3, C2.5).

A fourth, macro-level line of evidence is prominent but contested, and this
score does not depend on it. Household consumption is just under 40% of GDP,
compared with 60–70% in developed economies (CKGSB, 2026), and Michael
Pettis argues that China's growth model has been financed by transfers from
households through financial repression, a weak currency, and subsidies.
Others dispute the size of the gap: UBS economist Wang Tao estimates that
official methods for valuing owner-occupied housing understate consumption
by up to about 5% of GDP; Nicholas Lardy notes that counting social
transfers adds about 7 percentage points; and *The Economist* has rated
China's household disposable-income share of GDP ahead of several advanced
economies (as summarized by Premia Partners and Seafarer Funds).

Regulatory counterweights exist: a 44-hour statutory week, the 2021 court
ruling against "996," and stepped-up enforcement in 2025 (C2.1).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — extraction is
load-bearing through state-organized coerced labour and the absence of
independent collective bargaining. The finding holds even with the contested
financial-repression thesis set aside entirely.

**Domain 4 = 0.5 + 0.0 + 0.0 + 0.0 + 0.0 = 0.5/5 (10%)**

### Domain 5: Implementation Viability

#### C5.1 Proven Component Foundation: 1.0 (Pass)

**Rationale:** The system has operated continuously since 1978 — 48 years —
for a population of more than 1.4 billion (State Council Information
Office), with extensively documented outcomes (C1.1). It has also been
replicated: Vietnam's leaders launched the Đổi Mới reforms in 1986 believing
they were following a Chinese model, though they moved more gradually
(Bloomberg, via Facts and Details), and Vietnam's reform record shows
significant gains in growth, trade openness, poverty reduction, and human
development (Academia.edu review of Đổi Mới, 1986–2005).

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — the longest and largest track record
of any system scored in Step 1b, plus an independent replication. This
follows Status Quo's 1.0 reasoning — a certification of track record, not of
adequacy, which Domains 1–4 assess — and differs from CPS's 0.5, which
rested on that system's collapse.

#### C5.2 Staged Transition Pathways: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The reform sequence is one of the best-documented staged
transitions in economic history: local experiments first, national policy
afterward, sector by sector over decades (C3.4). Vietnam later adapted it,
and Ethiopia adopted a "developmental state" doctrine in the early 2000s
that drew on the East Asian tigers and, later, on China's industrial-park
and special-economic-zone model (*Jacobin*). Three limits keep this below a
Pass:

- **The pathway runs from central planning,** not from the pluralist market
  democracy that most prospective adopters would start from.
- **The model's defining precondition — a Leninist party-state — did not
  arise gradually.** In China it emerged from revolution and civil war,
  which is the reasoning behind CPS's 0.0 on this criterion. An analysis of
  Ethiopia's case notes that the Chinese example it followed replicated the
  party-state's central role (*Third World Quarterly*, 2018, "The Ethiopian
  developmental state").
- **No NEEC-formatted dual plan** — staged and rapid, with milestones —
  exists.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, replicated, well-documented
gradualist sequence, but no pathway from the starting point most adopters
would actually occupy.

#### C5.3 Partial and Parallel Deployability: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Components are adopted piecemeal and coexist with markets.
Within China, the private sector supplies most output and employment;
abroad, Vietnam had 343 industrial and economic zones by 2019 (Braumiller
Law Group), and Ethiopia built industrial parks on the Chinese pattern
(C5.2). But the comprehensive form — party-state direction of the economy —
requires comprehensive political control. The one formal arrangement for
coexistence, Hong Kong's "one country, two systems," has been dismantled
since the 2020 National Security Law (Freedom House).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — components deploy piecemeal, but
the comprehensive form requires near-total political control. This matches
the Nordic anchor for this band.

#### C5.4 Political Coalition Potential: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Measured domestic support is high, with documented caveats.

- **Direct surveys.** Harvard's Ash Center interviewed more than 31,000
  people between 2003 and 2016. Satisfaction with the central government
  rose over that period to roughly 93–95% in 2016, depending on the measure
  reported, while only 11.3% were "very satisfied" with township government
  (*Harvard Gazette*; *China Daily*). Jason Hickel, a scholar on the left,
  cites 2024 survey data in which 79% of Chinese respondents describe their
  country as democratic.
- **Preference falsification.** List experiments suggest that direct
  questions overstate regime support by 24.5–26.5 points (Robinson &
  Tannenberg, *Research & Politics*, 2019) or by 28.5 points on average
  (Carter et al., *The China Quarterly*, 2024) — leaving underlying support
  around 60% on the more skeptical estimate. Wenfang Tang's list experiments
  found no falsification, though with anonymity limitations that later
  authors note.
- **Durability.** The system has lasted nearly five decades and survived
  several leadership transitions.
- **External appeal and resistance.** Many governments back Beijing
  diplomatically — the 2022 Human Rights Council motion to debate the
  Xinjiang report failed (C4.3) — and developmental states have emulated the
  model (C5.2). In pluralist democracies, however, the one-party requirement
  is incompatible with coalition-building across the political spectrum.

The threshold's own terms — support across the political spectrum and
opposition to repeal — presuppose an open spectrum and a lawful path to
repeal, neither of which exists here. Measured support is therefore
inherently confounded.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — falsification-adjusted domestic
support near 60%, demonstrated durability, and real developing-world appeal,
set against structural incompatibility with pluralist politics. This is
materially stronger than CPS's 0.0, which rested chiefly on a catastrophic
record and collapse. **Flagged as contestable:** weighting the
incompatibility with democratic coalition-building more heavily gives 0.0.
Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 9 failures, tier unchanged.

#### C5.5 Cultural Adaptability: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Working versions exist in culturally proximate Vietnam
(C5.1). The clearest cross-cultural test, Ethiopia, made rapid progress on
nearly every available socioeconomic indicator and launched a series of
mega-projects. It then saw mass protests from 2014 and an unraveling of
elite cohesion after Prime Minister Meles Zenawi's death in 2012, and the
ruling coalition was dissolved in 2019 (*Ethiopia's "Developmental State":
Political Order and Distributive Crisis*, open-access book, University of
Manchester Global Development Institute, 2023). The model contains no
explicit mechanism for adapting to different cultural contexts.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — real transfer, with fragile and
mixed results outside the Sinitic sphere. This matches CPS's 0.5.

**Domain 5 = 1.0 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 3.0/5 (60%)**

## Final Assessment

**Key Strengths:**

- **C5.1 and C3.2 are both well-evidenced Passes:** the largest and longest
  real-world track record of any Step 1b system, with an independent
  replication in Vietnam, and a real-world stress test of inflation control
  passed in 2022.
- **Domain 1 (3.0/6) is the highest of the six Step 1b systems, and China is
  the first Step 1b system with no Domain 1 failure.** Every Material
  Security criterion lands at Partial, on the strength of broad home
  ownership, state housing and employment levers, and a wealth Gini in the
  social-democratic band.
- **Real state capacity shows up across Domain 3 and C1.4:** fast crisis
  mobilization, directed employment absorption, and the ability to absorb
  shocks through state-controlled finance.

**Key Deficiencies:**

- **Domain 2 (1.0/5) is the second-lowest in the prospective corpus, above
  only CPS, and Domain 4 (0.5/5) ties Status Quo for the lowest.** Five of
  the eight failures (C2.1, C2.2, C2.4, C4.4, C4.5) are undisputed under
  every reading this evaluation considered.
- **C3.5:** suppression of failure information is documented across
  epidemic, disaster, economic, and mortality data — frequently by the
  state's own later investigations.
- **C4.3:** UN and ILO findings on Xinjiang, a new assimilationist law, and
  declining top-level representation of women outweigh a real history of
  affirmative policy.
- **C4.2:** coal expansion and relative targets outweigh, for now, the
  world's largest renewable buildout.
- Eight failures ties Doughnut Economics for the most among the six Step 1b
  systems.

**A new exact-tie finding.** At 10.0/26, this system ties **Centrally
Planned Socialism** and **Stakeholder Capitalism** exactly: a three-way tie
within one tier, with three different failure counts (8, 12, and 9). The CPS
tie is the more revealing, because CPS is the same country's pre-reform
system. The two share identical Domain 1 (3.0) and Domain 3 (2.5) totals,
reached through different criteria, and differ on 15 of 26 criteria overall.
In NEEC's terms, reform traded Ethical Integrity (−2.5) for Implementation
Viability (+2.0) and a small gain in Human Autonomy (+0.5), while
reshuffling rather than raising the Material Security and System Resilience
totals. The two systems share only four failures (C2.1, C2.2, C2.4, C4.4).
This is the corpus's clearest illustration yet that an identical total can
conceal a radically different profile — a companion to the Georgism/UBS tie,
which illustrates a different point (an identical total landing in different
tiers).

**A structural-archetype finding.** None of the three shapes named during
Step 1b fits this system. It is not a single mechanism layered onto a market
economy (Georgism, Mutual Credit/LETS, Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism), a
comprehensive vision without committed mechanisms (Doughnut Economics), or a
federation of committed sector mechanisms (UBS). It is a whole existing
national political economy, like the Report's Systems 1–5. The Step 1b
taxonomy was built from reform proposals and mechanisms; the insertion and
regeneration passes should decide whether China — and later Singapore and
the Gulf states — belong with Systems 1–5 in the Report's presentation even
though they will be numbered after them.

**Comparison to the corpus (every formal claim checked by
`verify_china.py`).** Only CCO-PTF-CIP-SZH dominates this system, and this
system dominates no other. It forms non-dominated pairs with CPS, Status
Quo, Stakeholder Capitalism, Nordic Social Democracy, and Sovereign Wealth
Fund Statism. In the prospective 19-system corpus it is tied for 16th–18th,
ahead of only Libertarian Minarchism (8.0/26).

**Notes for the sibling sub-entries.** The scope logic used here — score the
system as currently configured, disclose where the historical record
differs, and discount contested official statistics — should carry over to
Singapore. The Gulf sub-entry still needs its own full evidence-gathering
