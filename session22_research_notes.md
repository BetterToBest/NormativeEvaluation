# Session 22 research notes — Islamic finance / profit-sharing banking

Written incrementally during the research turn, per Handoff 21's instruction.
Every item below is from a cited source retrieved this session; nothing here
rests on training data unless marked ORIENTATION.

## 1. The design (mechanism definition)

- PLS = profit-and-loss sharing: **mudarabah** (one party supplies capital,
  the other management; profit shared by pre-agreed ratio, loss borne by the
  capital provider absent negligence) and **musharakah** (both contribute
  capital; profit by agreed ratio, loss by capital share). Wikipedia "Profit
  and loss sharing", AIMS Education.
- Mohammad Nejatullah Siddiqi proposed a **two-tier mudarabah model** as the
  basis of riba-free banking, supplemented by fixed-return modes (murabaha
  markup, ijara leasing, salam, istisna). *"In practice, the fixed-return
  models — in particular murabaha — have become the bank's favourites, as
  long-term financing with profit-and-loss-sharing mechanisms has turned out
  to be more risky and costly than the long term or medium-term lending of
  the conventional banks."* (Wikipedia, citing Yousef and others.)
- Murabaha: cost-plus sale, cost and markup disclosed; asset-backed but **no
  risk sharing**. AIMS: "Compared with Musharakah, Murabaha does not involve
  shared business equity and profit loss participation."
- Diminishing musharakah (musharakah mutanaqisah): bank and customer jointly
  buy; customer rents the bank's share and buys it out over time. Used for
  home financing; RHB Islamic pioneered it in Malaysia.

## 2. The practice-versus-design gap (THE central disclosure)

- **Malaysia, tawarruq share of Islamic bank financing:** 22.4% in 2016 (BNM
  Islamic Finance Development Report 2016), **46% at end-2019** (BNM 2020b),
  per Ahmad et al., ISRA International Journal of Islamic Finance 12(2),
  2020 (Emerald). Tawarruq = commodity murabaha; a markup/debt structure,
  not PLS.
- Malaysian bank-by-bank contract preferences: Maybank Islamic "Commodity
  Murabahah-led financing"; CIMB Islamic "Islamic First Tawarruq"; Bank
  Muamalat "Tawarruq-based financing"; Islamic credit cards converting to
  tawarruq post-BNM 2025–2026 (shariabanking.com — secondary, low-tier).
- **BNM itself treats risk-sharing as underused.** AR2025, ch1d: BNM
  launched the **i-CITA programme in September 2025** to "promote wider use
  of risk-sharing contracts, such as *mudarabah* and *musyarakah*," with
  **RM100 million of matching funds**, because "economic activities with
  longer horizons and unpredictable cashflows ... cannot rely on traditional
  debt alone." An October 2025 BNM Shariah roundtable discussed "using
  Shariah contracts **beyond tawarruq**."
- **IFSB IFSI Stability Report 2025** (13th edn, "Navigating Shallow
  Waters"): capital and leverage sound overall, "though rising
  risk-weighted assets and **heavy reliance on hybrid instruments such as
  commodity murābaḥah in some jurisdictions point to emerging
  vulnerabilities** that require ongoing supervisory vigilance."
- **El-Gamal, *Islamic Finance: Law, Economics and Practice* (CUP 2006):**
  the industry has "placed excessive emphasis on contract forms, thus
  becoming a primary target for rent-seeking legal arbitrageurs"; Shari'a
  arbitrage = "identifying a captive market, with religious injunctions that
  forbid a given set of financial products and services, and synthesizing
  those products and services from variations on those premodern nominate
  contracts." Agency innovations in murabaha "practically eliminate risks of
  ownership and yet continue to justify return based on that cosmetic risk."
  Critical of Shari'a supervisory boards; advocates mutuality instead.
- Literature review (Contemporary Review of Islamic Finance and Accounting
  Literature, 2019) groups the critiques: form/substance divergence
  (El-Gamal 2006), convergence with conventional products (Ayub 2007),
  cost and inefficiency, neglect of socio-economic and environmental
  objectives (Kamla and Rammal 2013; Kuran 1983, 1995).

## 3. Malaysia — the principal implementation case

- **BNM Annual Report 2025, ch1d** (primary): "Islamic financing rose to
  **48% of total financing** and **takaful participation reached 24.5%**."
- Earlier trajectory (secondary, DDCAP citing BNM ARs): financing share
  44.5% (2022) → 45.6% (2023); Islamic banking AUM RM1,236.3bn (2021) →
  RM1,316.2bn (2022). Successive governments targeted **50% parity by 2025**;
  dual system since 1983 (Bank Islam Malaysia Berhad, first in SE Asia).
- 16 licensed full-fledged Islamic banks under the Islamic Financial
  Services Act 2013 (11 domestic, 5 foreign), plus AEON Bank, first digital
  Islamic bank, licensed 2024 (secondary).
- **Value-Based Intermediation (VBI):** IFIs intermediated **RM148.6
  billion** into VBI-aligned activities in 2024 (SMEs, green financing,
  social finance). Anchored on *maqasid* Shariah — BNM's own footnote:
  "preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage and wealth (property)."
- **Social finance:** iTEKAD (microentrepreneurs) expanded in 2025 with
  **iTEKAD Protection**, a social takaful solution; four takaful operators
  committed at launch, two rolling out from December 2025. November 2025
  Policy Document on Broader Application of *Ta`awun* in Takaful.
- **Greening Halal Businesses (GHB):** carbon tracking tools for SMEs; with
  eight IFIs, over 200 halal SMEs have begun adopting greener practices.
- **MYOR-i**, the world's first transaction-based Islamic benchmark rate
  (2022), to be **mandated for Islamic products from H2 2027**; KLIBOR
  ceases by 1 January 2029.
- BNM is reviewing industry feedback on the **Shariah Contract Framework**
  and **Investment Account** discussion papers (open at evaluation date).
- Capacity export: BNM supported 17 jurisdictions in 2025 and ran the
  inaugural Islamic Finance Capacity Building Programme for Central Asia and
  Azerbaijan — evidence for C5.5 (cultural adaptability) and C5.3.

## 4. Global scale

- **IFSB 2025:** total IFSI assets **USD 3.88 trillion in 2024**, +14.9%
  y/y; Islamic banking assets +17.05%, Islamic insurance +16.9%, sukuk
  issuance +25.6%. Africa and Central Asia the fastest-growing regions.
  Structural vulnerability flagged: underdevelopment of capital markets and
  the insurance sector constrains scalability.
- IFSB PSIFIs data cover Islamic banks and windows in Afghanistan,
  Bangladesh, Indonesia, Kyrgyz Republic, Malaysia, Oman, Pakistan, Saudi
  Arabia, UAE, plus stand-alone Islamic banks in Bahrain, Brunei, Iraq,
  Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Nigeria, Palestine, Qatar, Sudan,
  Türkiye and the UK — i.e. the mechanism is operating in 20+ jurisdictions.
- IFSB counts **PSIAs inside "deposits"** alongside remunerative murabaha
  and commodity murabaha and non-remunerative current/wadi`ah accounts —
  relevant to the stock-versus-flow question (Session 17 sub-distinction).
- Refinitiv IFDI 2021 projected USD 4.94tn by 2025 (secondary, superseded by
  the IFSB figure above; do not use as an outturn).

## 5. Crisis resilience

- **IMF WP/10/201, Hasan and Dridi, "The Effects of the Global Crisis on
  Islamic and Conventional Banks":** features of the Islamic banking business
  model limited the adverse impact on profitability in 2008; risk-management
  weaknesses at some Islamic banks produced a *larger* profitability decline
  in 2009; Islamic banks' credit and asset growth outperformed in 2008-09,
  "contributing to financial and economic stability"; external rating
  agencies' reassessment was generally more favourable. IMF Survey (2010)
  puts global Islamic banking near USD 820 billion at end-2008.
- **Beck, Demirguc-Kunt et al. (2013),** as summarised in IMF WP/15/41:
  during the crisis Islamic banks had a higher intermediation ratio, higher
  asset quality, were better capitalised and recorded better stock-market
  performance. IMF WP/15/41 is titled "Are Islamic Banks More Resilient
  during Financial Panics?"

## 6. Inflation and monetary policy

- **IMF WP/16/72, "Monetary Policy in the Presence of Islamic Banking":**
  except Iran and Sudan, the countries with a significant Islamic segment run
  dual systems; their experience "shows that it is possible to have low and
  stable inflation and a functioning transmission mechanism for monetary
  policy in the presence of Islamic banks." Brunei, Bahrain and Malaysia
  recorded the lowest inflation; Iran, Sudan, Pakistan and Yemen were
  identified as needing a stronger monetary policy framework. Conclusion for
  C3.2: the inflation record tracks the monetary regime, not the contract
  type.

## 7. Governance: the liability side of the gap

- **IMF WP/15/120, "An Overview of Islamic Finance":** "investment account
  holders generally have no control over the PER and IRR usage and, in some
  cases, are not informed of the Islamic bank's practices of maintaining
  these reserves." PER is allocated from operating income for smoothing
  before the bank's (mudarib's) share; IRR is set aside from the account
  holders' income share as a cushion for future losses.
- **IFSB Working Paper 10 (30 May 2019), risk-sharing in Islamic banking:**
  (a) "the UPSIA holders' lack of governance rights is well-noted by both the
  RSAs and the Islamic banks. However, neither consider indirect monitoring
  by the shareholders sufficient to make up for the lack of governance rights
  of the UPSIA holders"; (b) banks "comply mostly with the disclosure
  requirements relating to the utilisation of" PER and IRR; (c) **"high
  regulatory risk weights required on mudarabah and musharakah assets
  (excluding diminishing musharakah for home purchase finance) discourage
  Islamic banks from placing funds in such assets."** (c) is the causal
  mechanism behind the practice-versus-design gap.
- **Archer and Karim, via *Journal of Banking Regulation* (2009):** PSIA
  holders have no right to appoint or dismiss directors and no other "voice"
  in governance; unrestricted PSIA holders also have no choice over the
  risk-return characteristics of the assets their funds go into. IFSB's
  corporate-governance principles proposed a Governance Committee to supply
  the voice.
- **IFSB Guidance Note 3** (smoothing) and **BNM Guidelines on Profit
  Equalisation Reserve** (2011) set the disclosure and calibration rules.
  Displaced commercial risk: when PSIA returns are uncompetitive, account
  holders withdraw, so banks smooth payouts toward market returns.

## 8. Whole-system and transitioning cases

- **Iran:** Law for Usury (Interest)-Free Banking Operations passed 1983,
  interest-free banks operating from 21 March 1984; all banking activity
  Shariah-compliant as a matter of law. An IKAM country report states that
  interest-based transactions nonetheless persist, and that Iranian mudarabah
  often specifies the bank's profit in advance regardless of the venture's
  outcome.
- **Sudan:** Islamisation of the financial sector in documented phases from
  1983-85, 1985-89 onward; the IKAM report calls Sudan the only country to
  complete the project for its entire financial sector.
- **Pakistan:** Federal Shariat Court judgment of April 2022 (Justice Dr Syed
  Muhammad Anwer) requiring riba's elimination within five years; **26th
  Constitutional Amendment (October 2024)** rewrote Article 38(f) to fix the
  deadline at **1 January 2028**; State Bank working through a Committee for
  Transformation of Conventional Banking into Islamic; Ministry of Finance
  roadmap reported in 2026, with existing conventional contracts running to
  maturity. Secondary reporting notes Pakistan and Sudan had earlier reverted
  to dual systems.

## 9. Financial inclusion (sources conflict -- disclosed under C1.1)

- World Bank WPS6642 (Demirguc-Kunt, Klapper, Randall): "just 7 percent of
  unbanked Muslims and unbanked non-Muslims cite religion as a barrier";
  Muslims are nonetheless significantly less likely to own or save at a
  formal account after controls. Also: per a 2008 PwC Malaysia report, **the
  majority of Islamic finance customers in Malaysia are non-Muslim.**
- World Bank blog (2011 Findex): ~4% outside MENA, ~12% in MENA; more than 19
  million MENA adults avoid formal institutions for religious reasons; MENA
  account ownership 18% of adults.
- Brookings: 13% in selected Muslim-majority countries; cost was the larger
  barrier (over a fifth of the unbanked).
- World Bank (Increasing Financial Inclusion in the Muslim World, citing
  Demirguc-Kunt et al. 2015): "about 25 percent of adults reported religious
  reasons as a barrier."

## 10. Equity-financing share, and why it is low

- BIS-hosted address on Malaysian Islamic finance: "In Malaysia and in the
  Middle East, however, more than 70% are debt-based financing"; 71% of
  Islamic financing is fixed-rate against 26% in conventional.
- Literature (Islam and Ahmad 2020, via a musharakah mutanaqisah review):
  **no Malaysian microfinance institution offers musharakah or mudarabah**;
  microfinance uses tawarruq/commodity murabaha and qardhul hasan.
- Warninda, Ekaputra and Rokhim (2019), *Research in International Business
  and Finance* 49: across 63 Islamic banks, credit risk peaks when musharakah
  reaches roughly 37-39% of total financing -- i.e. the observed shares sit
  far below that.
- Musharakah mutanaqisah (diminishing partnership) is the exception that
  scaled, in home financing; RHB Islamic pioneered it in Malaysia.

## 11. Ecological instruments

- Securities Commission Malaysia **SRI Sukuk Framework, August 2014**;
  revised 2019 and August 2022 (SRI-Linked Sukuk Framework); Malaysia's first
  green sukuk 2017 (Tadau Energy); 13 SRI sukuk issuances by April 2020; SRI
  Sukuk and Bond Grant Scheme (2018, expanded 2022) covering up to 90% of
  external review costs to a maximum of RM300,000 per issuance; income-tax
  exemptions YA2021-YA2025. Framework developed with BNM and the World Bank.
- Context: Malaysia generated only about 3% of its power from renewables
  (Capital Monitor, citing the period around 2023); Petronas is among Asia's
  largest oil producers; IRENA estimates USD 375-415 billion of transition
  investment needed for the 2050 goals. Conclusion for C4.2: proceeds-use
  labelling, not an absolute constraint.

## 12. Gender

- BNM (2019), as reported in the *Journal of Asian Finance, Economics and
  Business*: Malaysian women entrepreneurs have insufficient participation in
  acquiring Islamic micro-financing relative to men. Micro-enterprises are
  77% of Malaysian SMEs; about 19.7% of SMEs are women-owned.
- INCEIF (Mahadi, Mazli and Muneeza 2019), *International Journal of
  Management and Applied Research* 6(3): documents the gender gap in Islamic
  wealth management and financial inclusion in the Malaysian context.
- Cross-country evidence that female-managed firms are less likely to obtain
  a bank loan and are charged more when approved is general, not specific to
  Islamic finance.

## 13. Zakat and waqf (scored as a scope scenario, not as the mechanism)

- Malaysia's zakat is state-coordinated (Zakat Collection Centres under state
  Islamic Religious Councils; JAWHAR for waqf, zakat and hajj), with tax
  deductions for payers; comparative work finds Malaysia's centralised model
  more efficient at collection than Indonesia's decentralised one.
- The literature reviews themselves report a gap in rigorous effectiveness
  estimates, and note fragmentation, capacity limits and distribution delays
  as recurring obstacles. Treat zakat's poverty impact as real but not
  well-quantified.
