## 17 [pending renumbering]. Universal Basic Services

### Overview

**Universal Basic Services (UBS)** is the proposal that a defined set of
goods and services essential to a decent life — healthcare, education,
housing, food, transport, legal/democratic participation, and basic
information access — should be provided free at the point of use (or
functionally decommodified) to the whole population, funded collectively
through general taxation rather than paid for individually through wages.
The term and its first fully-costed articulation originate with University
College London's Institute for Global Prosperity (IGP): the 2017 report
*Social Prosperity for the Future: A Proposal for Universal Basic Services*
(Henrietta L. Moore, Andrew Percy, Jonathan Portes, and Howard Reed) costed
an expansion of UK provision across shelter, food, transport, and
information, building on healthcare and education as sectors already
provided close to universally in the UK. A 2019 literature review (Anna
Coote, Pritika Kasliwal, and Andrew Percy, also IGP) formalized the
now-standard **seven-sector** framing — healthcare, education, democracy
and legal services, shelter, food, transport, and information — and Coote
and Percy expanded the case into a full book, *The Case for Universal Basic
Services* (Polity Press, 2020). Later work by the same core research
lineage (Ian Gough's "sufficiency economy" writing; a 2023 Hot or Cool
Institute paper reframing UBS within a "fair consumption space") extends
the argument's ecological dimension and, in some later formulations, adds
early-years care/childcare as an explicit eighth sector.

**Scope decision.** This evaluation scores the comprehensive, multi-sector
UBS proposal as specified across the IGP lineage (the 2017 report, the 2019
literature review, and the 2020 book) — not a single already-existing
universal service considered in isolation (the UK's National Health
Service alone, or a single city's free bus program alone). The distinction
matters throughout: several of UBS's seven named sectors already exist,
independently of UBS-the-framework, as separate, long-running, large-scale
real-world programs elsewhere in the world (universal healthcare in dozens
of countries; near-universal free primary/secondary education almost
everywhere; Vienna's century-old social housing model; Quebec's
28-year-old universal childcare program; free or heavily subsidized transit
in several cities and one full country). UBS's own distinctive claim is
that bundling all of these into one deliberately coordinated, universally
accessible package — rather than leaving each to develop separately, means-
tested, or not at all — produces a qualitatively different, more resilient,
and more equitable floor than any one sector alone. A reader evaluating one
of UBS's constituent sectors in isolation (a national health service on its
own, or a city's fare-free transit program on its own) should not import
this evaluation's scores wholesale; several criteria below (most clearly
C1.2a, C1.2b, and C4.4) turn specifically on features of the *comprehensive,
UBS-branded* package rather than on any one sector standing alone.

**Evidentiary tier (Appendix H.6) — mixed, and structured differently from
this project's other targeted-mechanism systems.** Unlike Doughnut
Economics (which, per its own Session 14 evaluation, deliberately declines
to commit to any single redistributive mechanism, naming problems while
citing others' solutions approvingly), UBS *does* commit to specific,
costed mechanisms of its own for each named sector — a UK-specific costing
of shelter (1.5 million new zero-rent social housing units, financed via
30-year Treasury bonds), food (covering roughly a third of meals for 2.2
million food-insecure households), transport (free bus travel for
everyone), and information (basic phone, broadband, and BBC licence-fee
access for everyone), on top of the already-universal health and education
sectors, at a total additional cost estimated at £42 billion per year
(roughly 2.3% of UK GDP as of the 2017 costing), proposed to be funded by
reducing the income-tax Personal Allowance. This is a genuinely different
evidentiary situation from Georgism's (one committed mechanism: a land
value tax paired with a citizen's dividend) or Mutual Credit/LETS's (one
committed mechanism: zero-sum, capped, endogenous credit) — UBS commits to
*several* separate, sector-specific mechanisms rather than one. Each
individual sector has real, and in some cases decades-long, real-world
precedent somewhere in the world; the *specific, coordinated, seven-sector
UK package* as costed by IGP has never itself been implemented anywhere.
This gap — genuinely strong separate-sector evidence, no integrated
implementation — recurs across several criteria below and, as with
Georgism, is the single most important thing to understand about this
evaluation before reading the individual scores.

### Domain 1: Material Security

#### C1.1 Poverty Elimination Capacity: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS's sector list maps closely onto the components a
cost-of-living-adjusted poverty measure would actually capture — housing,
food, healthcare, transport, and information access are not incidental to
material deprivation, they largely constitute it. The 2017 costing is
explicit that the resulting "social wage" is distributed progressively:
the poorest households benefit most in proportion to income, since a fixed
package of free services is worth more, relatively, to someone with less
income to begin with. Against this: the headline maximum benefit figure
circulated for the proposal (on the order of £126 per week if a household
used every service) applies in practice mainly to the subset of households
that would receive the new social housing allocation (roughly 1.5 million
of the UK's ~28 million households) — a critique the P2P Foundation's own
hosted commentary on the report makes directly, noting the benefit drops
to a much smaller figure (on the order of £39/week) for households outside
that subset. No jurisdiction has implemented the comprehensive package, so
there is no measured elimination *rate* to cite, and even the theoretical
maximum is unevenly distributed across the population it is meant to
reach.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, well-costed mechanism
targeting the actual constituents of material deprivation with a genuinely
progressive distributional profile, but no comprehensive-package
elimination-rate evidence exists anywhere, and the proposal's own most
widely-cited benefit figure is real only for a specific subset of
recipients rather than the population at large.

#### C1.2a Wealth Building for Resilience: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** This criterion asks whether participants can build a
$70,000+/20-year household buffer through the system's own mechanism.
UBS provides in-kind services, not cash, and — critically — even its
single most wealth-adjacent component (the shelter sector) does not build
resident equity: the 2017 costing proposes *zero-rent* social housing
allocated on a needs basis, which eliminates a major expense for
recipients but creates no ownership stake, deed, or capital account for
them to accumulate, unlike a Community Land Trust (where the resident
still holds real, if deed-restricted, home equity) or a cooperative capital
account (where a member's stake genuinely grows with contribution). This
is a meaningfully different, and weaker, case than Degrowth's own C1.2a
anchor for "collectively... held" wealth mechanisms (Appendix H.7v2): a
CLT resident has an asset; a UBS social-housing tenant has a service.
Reducing a major expense is a real benefit to disposable income, but — per
the same reasoning already established for Georgism's Citizen's Dividend
and Mutual Credit/LETS's zero-sum balances — an expense reduction is not,
by itself, an asset-accumulation vehicle unless specifically structured as
one, and no part of the UBS proposal is.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — no accumulation
mechanism exists anywhere in the proposal, for any participant; the
shelter sector's zero-rent design is a genuine, real expense-elimination
benefit but not, in itself, an equity-building one, matching this
criterion's now well-established corpus pattern (Integral, UBI, Georgism,
Mutual Credit/LETS, Doughnut Economics all fail here for closely related
reasons).

#### C1.2b Prevention of Exploitative Accumulation: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as a genuinely contestable score

**Rationale:** This criterion asks whether the system structurally prevents
wealth *concentration* — a Gini-coefficient-relevant question, distinct
from C1.2a's resilience-buffer question. This is the single most
consequential call in this evaluation, and it was revised once during
drafting, disclosed here rather than silently settled. **The case for 0.5**
(this evaluation's initial read): the shelter sector's plan to double the
UK's social housing stock by 1.5 million units removes that many households
from the private rental market entirely, a real, costed, committed
mechanism — unlike Doughnut Economics, which was scored 0.0 here
specifically for citing compatible redistributive mechanisms without
adopting any of its own, UBS *does* adopt a specific mechanism of its own,
which seemed to warrant credit above Doughnut Economics' clean zero, on
the model of Georgism's own 0.5 for land value capture. **Why this
evaluation lands at 0.0 instead:** on closer comparison with Georgism's
actual mechanism, the analogy does not hold. Georgism's land value tax
*captures wealth that would otherwise accumulate to landowners* — it is a
device that removes an existing, ongoing accumulation from one party's
hands. UBS's shelter sector does the opposite kind of thing: it *builds new
housing and gives it away at zero rent*, funded by general taxation. This
is real and valuable, but it does not capture, cap, or redistribute any
existing stock of wealth; it is an expenditure commitment, not a wealth-
concentration-prevention mechanism, and — like every other part of the
proposal — it says nothing about the Gini coefficient of the population's
existing asset holdings. The proposal's funding mechanism (reducing the
Personal Allowance) is a tax-and-transfer adjustment, not a wealth tax,
asset cap, or land-value-capture device either. On reflection, this
evaluation concludes UBS has no C1.2b mechanism of the kind this criterion
asks about, at any scale — a different, and weaker, case than either
Georgism's or Mutual Credit/LETS's own targeted mechanisms.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0), revised down from an
initial 0.5 read during drafting (see above) upon recognizing that
providing a new free service is not the same kind of act as capturing an
existing wealth stream — the distinction this project's own C1.2a/C1.2b
split exists to draw. Flagged as the evaluation's most genuinely
contestable single call: a future scorer weighing the housing plank's
supply-side downward pressure on private land/rental values more heavily
than this evaluation does could defensibly land at 0.5 instead, and this
is precisely the kind of close call the corpus's own disclosure norms ask
to be surfaced rather than silently resolved.

#### C1.3 Housing Security: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS's shelter sector is one of its most concretely specified
components: 1.5 million new social housing units, zero rent on a needs
basis, financed through 30-year Treasury bonds, exempted from Council Tax,
and including a utilities allowance. The closest full-scale real-world
analogue is Vienna's century-old social and cooperative housing system —
roughly 40–60% of Viennese residents live in municipally-owned or
subsidized cooperative housing (depending on how subsidized cooperative
units are counted alongside directly municipal ones), average rents run
well below comparable European capitals (on the order of €10.5/m² against
London's more than triple that figure), and the system traces continuously
to the "Red Vienna" municipal housing program begun in 1919. This is real,
genuine, multi-generational evidence that decommodified housing at
population scale is buildable and durable. Against this, two real
countervailing findings: first, independent critical assessments of Vienna
(including from the American Enterprise Institute and the Warsaw
Enterprise Institute, both writing from a skeptical, market-oriented
starting point) document that Vienna's own system, at scale, has drifted
toward disproportionately benefiting long-tenured, better-off households
under comparatively light means-testing — up to half of higher-income
Viennese households reportedly live in subsidized housing, and entry costs
for newer cooperative-unit residents (an upfront payment on the order of
$38,000, and rent roughly 17% higher than long-tenured neighbors in some
cases) are real and non-trivial. Second, the specific "zero rent on a needs
basis" design UBS itself proposes has never been implemented at the scale
proposed anywhere; Vienna's own system charges modest, not zero, rents,
funded via a dedicated payroll-style tax.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a well-specified, costed
mechanism with strong indirect real-world support (Vienna's century of
continuous operation), but not itself implemented anywhere at the scale or
on the exact terms proposed, and the closest full-scale analogue carries
genuine, documented access-equity critique that a full Pass would
understate.

#### C1.4 Automation Resilience: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Unlike Doughnut Economics — which names automation as a
concern but supplies no operational response and consequently fails this
criterion outright — UBS has a genuine, if partial, mechanism. The original
2017 report and its press coverage explicitly invoke automation risk as
motivation, citing the McKinsey estimate that roughly 49% of paid work
activities are technically automatable, representing on the order of $16
trillion in wages globally. UBS's actual response is structural rather than
compensatory: by removing housing, food, healthcare, education, transport,
and information costs from the household budget, it substantially lowers
the *amount of wage income* a person needs to avoid poverty, meaning a
given percentage loss of wages from automation translates into a smaller
loss of material security than it would under a system with no in-kind
floor. This is a real mechanism, structurally similar in kind to (though
narrower in scope than) Nordic Social Democracy's own unemployment-benefit
buffer. What UBS's proposal does *not* address at all is the demand side of
NEEC's own C1.4 threshold: since UBS provides no cash, it does nothing to
maintain aggregate market demand for goods and services outside its seven
sectors as wage income disappears — a household with zero income still has
no money for clothing, discretionary spending, or anything the seven
sectors don't cover. No stress-testing of the comprehensive UBS package
against NEEC's 30/50/70% displacement scenarios exists anywhere.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, explicitly-motivated,
better-than-average mechanism for the *income-need* side of the automation
problem (matching or exceeding several other 0.5-scoring systems in this
corpus on this specific point), but no mechanism at all for the aggregate-
demand side, and untested at any severity.

#### C1.5 Universal Wealth Access (narrowed — access breadth only): 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** This narrowed criterion asks about breadth of *access* to an
*active asset-growth mechanism* — not accumulation adequacy (C1.2a) or
concentration prevention (C1.2b). Access breadth would, in principle, be
a genuine strength of UBS: none of its seven sectors gate eligibility by
employment status, membership, or means test in the stigmatizing sense
(the entire universalist design premise is to avoid exactly that kind of
gate). But C1.2a's analysis already establishes that there is no
asset-growth mechanism at all for that openness to apply to — following
the identical reasoning already established for Integral, UBI, Georgism,
Mutual Credit/LETS, and Doughnut Economics, each of which fails this
criterion for the same underlying design-level reason once C1.2a fails.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — for the same reason as
C1.2a, not an access-breadth problem; access would be about as universal
as any system in this corpus if there were a mechanism to access.

**Domain 1 Total: 1.5/6 (25%)** — Three structural failures (C1.2a, C1.2b,
C1.5), all tracing to a single underlying finding worth stating plainly:
UBS is very good at *removing expenses* and only ever incidentally, if at
all, touches *existing wealth stocks*. The same shelter-sector mechanism
does different work across different criteria in this domain — it fails
C1.2a (no equity created for the resident) and, on reflection, C1.2b (no
wealth captured from anyone else) — while still contributing real, if
partial, credit to C1.3 (a genuine housing-security mechanism, evaluated on
its own terms). This is exactly the kind of criterion-differentiation the
C1.2a/C1.2b split (Section 12.1) was designed to expose, now visible for a
fourth distinct underlying reason (Integral: architectural exclusion;
Georgism: the mechanism is an income vehicle; Mutual Credit/LETS: no
value-storage function of any kind; UBS: real expenditure on services,
never wealth capture).

### Domain 2: Human Autonomy

#### C2.1 Freedom from Coercion: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** By removing housing, food, healthcare, transport, education,
and information costs from what a household must earn to survive, UBS
reduces one real, substantial form of "work or starve" coercion — a
mechanism of the same general kind that earns Nordic Social Democracy and
Universal Basic Income full Passes elsewhere in this corpus. What holds
UBS below a full Pass is a genuine, well-documented concern raised
directly within UBS's own literature: because the benefit is delivered
in-kind rather than as cash, it is not fungible, and a person whose needs
don't match the standard menu of services on offer (someone needing to
relocate for work and losing access to a specific city's housing
allocation, for instance) has less flexibility than an equivalent cash
transfer would provide — a "paternalism" or "consumer sovereignty"
critique that Anna Coote's own writing engages directly rather than
dismisses. UBS's core costed proposal also lacks the kind of substantial
cash unemployment-insurance component that gives Nordic Social Democracy
much of its own coercion-reducing strength; the 2017 report discusses a
modest cash supplement only as a possible complement, not as a load-bearing
element of the costed package this evaluation scores.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, substantial reduction in
survival-based coercion across several major cost categories, offset by a
genuine, corpus-relevant, and self-acknowledged (within UBS's own
literature) non-fungibility concern that a comparably-generous cash system
would not share.

#### C2.2 Labor Non-Necessity: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** NEEC's own threshold asks for "unconditional provision
covering 100% of basic needs (housing, food, healthcare, utilities) for
all residents" — language that maps almost directly onto UBS's own named
sectors. Several UBS sectors genuinely clear an unconditional-access bar
for the whole population (universal healthcare and education already
function this way in the UK; free transport and basic information access
are proposed as flatly universal, with no means test). But the shelter and
food sectors, as actually costed, are targeted rather than flatly
universal in the strongest sense: the housing plank provides zero-rent
housing only to households allocated it on a needs basis (roughly 1.5
million of the UK's households, not all of them), and the food plank
covers only a portion of meals for a defined food-insecure population (2.2
million households), not free food for every resident regardless of need.
This is an important, and easy to miss, distinction: UBS's "universal"
branding means "universally *available without stigma or means-testing
bureaucracy* to anyone who needs it," not "everyone receives the same
thing whether they need it or not" the way Universal Basic Income or
CCO-PTF's baseline does. This is a real, substantive design difference
from a clean unconditional-baseline system.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — genuinely unconditional for
several sectors (transport, information, and the pre-existing health/
education base) but explicitly needs-targeted, not flatly universal, for
the two sectors (shelter, food) most directly relevant to NEEC's own
threshold language, falling short of "100% of basic needs... for all
residents" as literally stated.

#### C2.3 Creative Development Opportunities: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS does not itself reduce working hours or fund cultural
programming the way Degrowth's explicit shorter-week design does. What it
does is indirect but real: removing major fixed costs (housing, transport,
some food, basic connectivity) frees disposable income and, for households
receiving free childcare-adjacent services in the sector's later
formulations, frees time as well — mirroring the same indirect-channel
reasoning already credited for Georgism and Mutual Credit/LETS on this
exact criterion. The information sector's inclusion of BBC licence-fee
access also has a genuine, if modest, direct cultural-access dimension.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a plausible, real, but indirect
effect through cost- and (in later formulations) time-freeing channels,
not a purpose-built mechanism comparable to systems designed explicitly
around this goal.

#### C2.4 Democratic Participation: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** This is a genuinely distinctive feature of UBS relative to
several already-scored fiscal/service mechanisms: "democracy and legal
services" is one of the seven named sectors, not an afterthought, and the
IGP lineage's own writing explicitly frames UBS as an opportunity to
"rejuvenate local democracy and local involvement in the design, financing
and delivery of local services." Ian Gough's own theoretical defense of UBS
against the paternalism critique rests specifically on this point: UBS, he
argues, is meant to *strengthen* local democratic control over which
services get built and how, not to impose a fixed menu from above,
explicitly countering critics who worry the framework prescribes rather
than empowers. Against this: unlike Doughnut Economics' City Portrait
methodology (which has actual, documented, multi-city participatory
workshop records — Barcelona, Glasgow, Brussels, and dozens more), UBS's
own participatory design intent has not been operationalized or evidenced
at comparable real-world scale; it remains closer to a stated design
principle than a demonstrated practice.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a genuine, explicitly-named design
commitment with a real theoretical defense, but, unlike Doughnut
Economics' own comparable claim on this criterion, not yet backed by a
comparable real-world participatory-practice record.

#### C2.5 Exit Rights and Mobility: 1.0 (Pass)

**Rationale:** UBS is layered onto whatever tax and governance system its
host country already runs, exactly as with Georgism, Mutual Credit/LETS,
and Doughnut Economics — it introduces no parallel currency or membership
system an individual would need to exit. Anyone can decline to use any or
all of UBS's services and pay privately for an equivalent instead (private
schooling and private healthcare already coexist with universal public
provision in the UK and elsewhere today), and relocating to a different
jurisdiction carries no penalty specific to UBS beyond whatever the
ordinary consequences of changing tax residency already are. This
criterion asks about individual exit and mobility, not about whether the
comprehensive package itself is modular across jurisdictions (that is
C5.3's question, addressed separately below).

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — no coercive lock-in at the individual
level by construction, matching the established pattern for every
targeted-mechanism system scored under the v2 structure so far.

**Domain 2 Total: 3.0/5 (60%)** — No structural failures. The pattern
across all five criteria is consistent with this project's other
targeted-mechanism evaluations: real, correctly-directed mechanisms,
evidenced at a level of generosity or specificity that falls short of
NEEC's demanding thresholds, with the "democracy and legal" sector
supplying UBS's own distinctive (if under-evidenced) contribution to this
domain.

### Domain 3: System Resilience

#### C3.1 Crisis Response Capacity: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS has no automatic benefit-scaling mechanism triggered by
crisis severity (no CCO-style automatic increase during a downturn). It
does have a real, structural advantage of a different kind: services that
are already running as a universal default — healthcare being the clearest
case — do not need to be newly activated, applied for, or means-tested
during a crisis, since the "trigger" already fired permanently the day the
service became universal. A related, independently-noted finding: research
connecting UBS's own sector list to actual pandemic-era policy (an LSE
Public Policy Review analysis) observes that the "essential worker"
designations used across many countries during COVID-19 map closely onto
UBS's own named sectors — a real, if indirect, validation that these
particular sectors are what a genuine crisis reveals as critical. Several
UBS-adjacent services were also directly expanded as emergency crisis
response during COVID specifically (Kansas City's full system-wide
zero-fare transit rollout, discussed further under C5.1 below, was
accelerated and completed in March 2020 as the pandemic began).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — no automatic scaling mechanism,
but the underlying sectors are structurally crisis-relevant by already
being in permanent operation, and there is real, if indirect, evidence
(the essential-worker overlap; COVID-era service expansions) connecting
UBS's specific sector choices to genuine crisis conditions.

#### C3.2 Inflation Control Mechanisms: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS has no monetary-policy innovation of its own and
inherits whatever the host country's central bank does — the same
reasoning already applied to Market Socialism, Stakeholder Capitalism,
Georgism, and Doughnut Economics. One narrow, real point worth crediting
specifically: for the roughly one-fifth to one-third of a typical
household budget UBS's covered sectors represent (housing, transport, and
a share of food), market-price inflation in those specific categories no
longer directly affects covered households' material welfare, since the
price to the user is fixed at zero regardless of the state's own
underlying procurement costs — a narrower, sector-specific version of the
"sectoral demand isolation" logic credited more fully to CCO-PTF elsewhere
in this corpus. This insulates household welfare in those categories
without insulating the state's own fiscal exposure to inflation in them.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — matches the established anchor
for "no innovation beyond conventional monetary policy," with a real,
narrow price-insulation effect for the specific covered categories
credited without inflating the overall score, since it does not amount to
a designed monetary-stability mechanism.

#### C3.3 Multi-Failure Resistance: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** No designed compound-crisis architecture exists (no
federation or redundancy comparable to Integral or CCO-PTF). A real, if
incidental, resilience property exists nonetheless: because housing,
healthcare, food (for the covered subset), transport, and information are
provided directly by the state rather than purchased entirely from private
markets funded by wage income, a private-market financial shock or supply
disruption does not automatically cut off access to these specific goods
the way it would for someone relying entirely on wages to buy market
equivalents — the same reasoning already credited to Georgism and Mutual
Credit/LETS on this exact criterion.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real but incidental resilience
property from decommodified provisioning in specific categories, not a
designed multi-failure architecture.

#### C3.4 Epistemic Adaptability: 1.0 (Pass)

**Rationale:** This is unusually well-evidenced by a genuine, dated,
decade-long record of the same core research group revising its own model
in response to critique and changing context. The 2017 costing (four new
sectors) was formalized and extended to seven named sectors by the 2019
literature review; expanded into a full book-length treatment in 2020;
extended further to explicitly incorporate an ecological "sufficiency"
framing by Gough and, in 2023, the Hot or Cool Institute's "fair
consumption space" reframing; and has continued to evolve through ongoing
engagement with the G7's own Think7 policy process, with IGP's Henrietta
Moore continuing as a lead author on social-cohesion policy briefs across
at least three separate G7 cycles (the German 2022 presidency, the
Japanese 2023 presidency, and a further brief dated into the 2026 cycle).
This is a real, citable, near-decade-long pattern of the framework's own
authors revising scope, framing, and emphasis in response to new evidence
and changing political context, comparable to or exceeding Doughnut
Economics' own strong C3.4 case in this corpus.

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — a rare, directly-evidenced,
near-decade-long record of iterative revision by the framework's own core
authors, spanning multiple distinct extensions and a sustained,
multi-cycle international-policy engagement.

#### C3.5 Failure-Mode Transparency: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Real transparency exists at the level of ordinary
democratic and academic scrutiny: Coote's own writing engages the
paternalism/consumer-sovereignty critique directly rather than avoiding it;
independent critical assessments of the closest real-world analogues
(Vienna's housing-access equity critique from AEI and the Warsaw
Enterprise Institute; Quebec childcare's well-publicized child-development
findings, discussed under C1.1/C4.3) are public, specific, and openly
debated, not suppressed. Kansas City's zero-fare transit reversal (C5.1,
below) is likewise a matter of public record, with funding shortfalls and
rising safety-incident data openly reported by local and national outlets
well before the program's 2026 end. What does not exist is any purpose-
built, systematic mechanism (comparable to Integral's FRS or CCO-PTF's own
dashboards) for surfacing failures across the UBS system as a whole; every
instance of transparency identified here comes from ordinary journalistic
and academic scrutiny of specific implementations, not a built-in feature
of UBS itself.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — real, ordinary-process-level
transparency across multiple specific implementations, with critiques
engaged rather than dismissed by the framework's own proponents, but no
purpose-built failure-detection architecture.

**Domain 3 Total: 3.0/5 (60%)** — One clean pass (C3.4, unusually
well-evidenced by a near-decade-long, multi-stage revision record), no
failures, four partials — closely matching the general shape already
established for Georgism, Mutual Credit/LETS, and Doughnut Economics in
this domain.

### Domain 4: Ethical Integrity

#### C4.1 Intergenerational Justice: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS's later ecological framing (Gough's sufficiency-economy
work; the 2023 Hot or Cool Institute paper) explicitly ties the proposal to
reduced future ecological burden, and a genuinely strong, current,
peer-reviewed empirical anchor exists for the underlying causal logic: a
2021 study in *Global Environmental Change* (Vogel, Steinberger, O'Neill,
Lamb, and Krishnakumar), analyzing 106 countries, finds that higher public
service quality is associated with greater human-need satisfaction *and*
lower energy requirements for achieving it, while economic growth and
extractivism are associated with the opposite pattern. This is real,
quantified, current support for the claim that decommodified, service-based
provisioning is compatible with — and possibly conducive to — reduced
resource burden on future generations. Against this, UBS has no
carbon-reduction *target* or intergenerational-transfer commitment of its
own the way CCO-PTF or Degrowth do; the ecological case rests on this
correlational (if increasingly well-evidenced) academic finding rather than
a designed structural commitment.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — strong, current, quantified
academic support for the underlying logic, but no specific measurable
intergenerational commitment built into the proposal itself.

#### C4.2 Ecological Compliance: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The evidence here is real and, in one specific instance,
directly quantified rather than merely argued: Luxembourg's 2020 move to
nationwide fare-free public transport has been independently studied using
a synthetic difference-in-differences design, finding an average reduction
in road-transport CO2 emissions of roughly 5.9%, robust to confounding
factors such as remote-work shifts and electric-vehicle adoption. This is
a genuine, quantified, causally-designed finding directly on point for
UBS's transport sector specifically. The Vogel et al. (2021) 106-country
finding cited under C4.1 supplies a broader, structural version of the same
argument. Against a full Pass: not every transit expansion produces this
result cleanly — Tallinn, Estonia's own free-transport program (the first
in a European capital, since 2013) raised ridership by roughly 14% in its
first year, but a substantial share of new trips were for leisure or social
purposes rather than substitutions for car trips, meaning the emissions
benefit is genuinely conditional on local circumstances rather than
automatic. More fundamentally, UBS has no hard ecological ceiling or
absolute-emissions-reduction target built into its own design the way
Degrowth's or CCO-PTF's do; its ecological benefit is a claimed, and now
partially evidenced, side effect of decommodified and shared provisioning,
not a designed structural constraint.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — real, current, in one case
directly quantified evidence for a genuine ecological co-benefit
(especially in the transport sector specifically), but no designed hard
constraint, and the one clean quantified case (Luxembourg) sits alongside
a real counter-example (Tallinn) showing the effect is not automatic.

#### C4.3 Racial and Gender Equity: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as a genuinely contestable score

**Rationale:** This is a closer call than most scores in this evaluation,
and is flagged explicitly, following this project's established practice
for genuinely uncertain criteria. **In favor of a stronger score:**
universal, non-means-tested provision has real, quantified, peer-reviewed
support for reducing a specifically *racially* disparate burden — a 2006
study (Stuber and Schlesinger, Columbia University, published in *Social
Science & Medicine*) finds that stigma associated with means-tested welfare
programs is "exacerbated by poor health and by minority status," meaning
removing means-testing specifically (as every UBS sector does) removes a
documented channel of racially disparate harm that a means-tested
alternative would not. Separately, universal (non-targeted) healthcare
coverage has been found, in at least one study of a military health
system, to mitigate racial disparities in maternal-complication outcomes
relative to a less universal comparator. On gender specifically, Quebec's
universal childcare program — a genuine, real-world precedent for one
UBS-relevant sector — produced a well-documented, substantial increase in
maternal labor-force participation (an estimated 70,000 additional mothers
employed by 2008) and is widely credited with narrowing the province's
gender pay gap. **Against a stronger score:** no explicit reparative
mechanism for historical racial wealth or opportunity gaps exists anywhere
in the UBS proposal, the same gap already identified for nearly every
system in this corpus except CCO-PTF, Participatory Economics, Degrowth,
MMT+JG, and UBI; and the clearest real-world rollout precedent (Quebec's
own phased age-band expansion of subsidized childcare between 1997 and
2000) is documented to have created early, genuine access advantages for
wealthier families who could better navigate securing a market-rate spot
while waiting for a subsidized one to open — a concrete, if not explicitly
racial, illustration of how "universal" access can still reproduce
inequality through ordinary rollout dynamics.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a genuinely strong, quantified
universalist case for reduced racially- and gender-disparate stigma and
outcomes, weighed against the standard corpus-wide absence of an explicit
racial-reparative mechanism and a real, documented rollout-equity concern
in the closest precedent; presented as a close call rather than a
confidently-resolved one, in the same spirit as Georgism's and Mutual
Credit/LETS's own flagged C4.3 scores.

#### C4.4 Power Distribution: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** UBS's funding mechanism (an income-tax adjustment) does
nothing to address wealth- or corporate-ownership-based power
concentration directly, and — per the revised C1.2b finding above — the
shelter sector's housing expansion does not function as a wealth-Gini-
relevant mechanism either. What does provide a real, if narrow, point of
contact with this criterion is the "democracy and legal" sector
specifically: universal access to legal aid, courts, and representation is
a classic and genuine access-to-justice equalizer, reducing the practical
power asymmetry between an ordinary citizen and a better-resourced
opposing party (a corporate landlord, a well-funded litigant) in exactly
the kind of dispute where unequal access to legal representation currently
functions as a form of power concentration in its own right. This is real
but narrow — it says nothing about corporate governance, campaign finance,
or ownership concentration more broadly, and NEEC's own C4.4 threshold also
names citizen-proposal-adoption rates and democratic accountability more
generally, dimensions C2.4 already establishes UBS does not evidence at
scale.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, if narrow, mechanism
(universal legal aid and access to courts) against one specific channel of
power asymmetry, silent on every other channel this criterion's own
threshold names.

#### C4.5 Exploitation Elimination: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** NEEC's own C4.5 rationale names three target relationships:
landlord-tenant, employer-employee, creditor-debtor. UBS's shelter sector
speaks directly, and — for the specific households it reaches — completely,
to the first: converting a household from a private-market renter into a
zero-rent public tenant removes that household entirely from any
landlord-tenant extraction relationship, a cleaner elimination for the
covered subset than Georgism's own land value tax achieves (which merely
taxes the unearned rent component while leaving the landlord-tenant
relationship, and some rent payment, intact). This completeness-for-the-
covered-subset is a genuinely different point from the wealth-Gini
question already addressed under C1.2b — a household can be fully removed
from an extractive relationship without this constituting a dent in
society-wide wealth concentration, and that is exactly the distinction this
criterion and C1.2b are designed to keep separate. Against a higher score:
UBS reaches only the subset of households actually allocated new social
housing (roughly 1.5 million, not the general population), and it says
nothing at all about the other two named relationships — no cooperative-
ownership mandate touches employer-employee extraction, and no credit
reform touches creditor-debtor extraction.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a genuinely complete, if narrow-
reach, elimination of one of the three named extraction channels for the
population it actually covers, with the other two channels entirely
untouched by design.

**Domain 4 Total: 2.5/5 (50%)** — No failures, uniform partials across all
five criteria — the same flat shape already seen for Georgism's own Domain
4, and for a similar underlying reason: one real, specific, well-targeted
mechanism (the shelter sector, here; land rent capture, for Georgism) shows
up as partial credit across several criteria, while everything the
mechanism doesn't touch sits at a flat, unremarkable 0.5.

### Domain 5: Implementation Viability

#### C5.1 Proven Component Foundation: 1.0 (Pass)

**Rationale:** This is one of UBS's genuinely strongest criteria, and — on
reflection during drafting — this evaluation credits it more highly than
an initial instinct toward a more cautious 0.5 (in the manner of Integral's
own "components proven, integration untested" case) would suggest. The
distinguishing question, per this project's own established practice
(compare CCO-PTF's and Mutual Credit/LETS's own 1.0 scores here): does the
system's *own* literature hedge on integration being untested (Integral's
own text explicitly does; UBS's does not), or does it present a specific,
confident costing? UBS's component base is unusually broad and
independently strong across multiple distinct sectors: universal
healthcare has operated in the UK's National Health Service for more than
75 years at national scale; universal free education is functionally
ancient; Vienna's social housing has run continuously for more than a
century at genuine city-wide scale; Quebec's universal childcare has run
for 28 years, reaching close to half the province's preschool population
at its peak and since expanded into a full-Canada, federally-funded
national program; and free or heavily subsidized transit has real, if more
recent and more mixed, precedent in Luxembourg, Tallinn, and (for a period)
Kansas City. Each of these clears NEEC's own Pass Threshold (≥20 years'
operation at scale ≥10,000 participants, with documented outcomes) on its
own terms, and the *diversity* of independently-proven sectors is
comparable to, or arguably exceeds, the component bases already credited
with a full Pass elsewhere in this corpus (CCO-PTF's alternative
currencies, CLTs, and sovereign wealth funds; Mutual Credit/LETS's WIR
Bank, Sardex, and Grassroots Economics).

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — an unusually broad, diverse,
multi-decade, large-scale component record spanning at least five of the
seven named sectors, matching the corpus's own established standard for
crediting a full Pass on component strength even where the fully-integrated
package has never itself been attempted; a more conservative scorer
weighting the entirely-untested comprehensive integration more heavily
could reasonably land at 0.5 instead, and this call is disclosed as
somewhat less than fully settled.

#### C5.2 Staged Transition Pathways: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** No detailed, multi-phase national timeline comparable to
CCO-PTF's own four-phase design exists for the comprehensive UBS package,
and no rapid (≤36-month) crisis-deployment protocol has been specified at
all. What does exist, and earns credit above a flat 0.0, is genuine,
real, staged precedent for *individual* sectors: Quebec's own childcare
program phased in by child age-band between 1997 and 2000 before reaching
close to its full covered population, and this specific, real, multi-year
staged rollout was later used directly as the explicit model for Canada's
national C$10-a-day childcare expansion (announced 2021, with C$37 billion
in further federal funding committed through 2031) — a genuine, dated,
real-world example of a UBS-relevant sector scaling from a single
sub-national pilot to a full-country program over roughly a quarter-century,
comparable in kind to Georgism's own citation of the Australian Capital
Territory's staged transition.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real, dated, sector-specific
staged-transition precedent for at least one of UBS's seven sectors,
comparable to other 0.5-scoring systems' own best evidence on this
criterion, but no comprehensive multi-sector plan and no rapid-deployment
protocol of any kind.

#### C5.3 Partial and Parallel Deployability: 1.0 (Pass)

**Rationale:** UBS is inherently and demonstrably modular: each of its
seven sectors has, in fact, been adopted independently by different
jurisdictions without the others being present. Free transit alone:
Luxembourg (nationwide), Tallinn, and (for a period) Kansas City. Universal
childcare alone: Quebec, later all of Canada. Universal healthcare alone:
dozens of countries, independent of whether they have UBS's other sectors.
This is directly demonstrated, real-world, repeated independent adoption
of individual components — arguably an even more concretely evidenced case
than Georgism's or Doughnut Economics' own Pass here, since UBS's
components have been adopted separately by genuinely different countries
for genuinely unrelated reasons, not merely as pilots of one unified
framework.

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — demonstrated, not merely claimed,
independent real-world adoption of individual components across multiple
distinct countries and contexts.

#### C5.4 Political Coalition Potential: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Real, current, and substantial evidence exists on both
sides of this criterion's threshold. In favor: the UK Labour Party's 2019
conference named UBS one of three central pillars of its economic program
(alongside ownership/democracy reform and a cash safety net), a genuine
endorsement by a major political party's leadership; the concept has
maintained sustained institutional traction at the international level,
with IGP's own researchers serving as continuing lead authors on the G7's
Think7 social-cohesion policy briefs across at least three separate
presidency cycles (Germany 2022, Japan 2023, and into the 2026 cycle); and
the underlying *principle* — universal, free-at-point-of-use, publicly
delivered essential services — commands strong and, as of 2024–2026,
*rising* cross-partisan UK public support for individual sectors
specifically (YouGov polling finds 76% support for rail nationalization,
including 60% of Conservative and 77% of Reform UK voters; 71% support for
energy nationalization, up from 53% in 2017; and separate polling finds
86–90% support for keeping the NHS in public, free-at-point-of-use form).
Against a stronger score: this polling measures support for individual,
already-familiar sectors rather than the specific "Universal Basic
Services" branded comprehensive package, which has not itself been polled
as a whole to this evaluation's knowledge; Labour's 2019 enthusiasm
predates that party's loss in the 2019 general election, so this evidence
should be read as historical political-coalition proof-of-concept rather
than confirmation of continuing current party policy; and Kansas City's
own 2026 reversal of its zero-fare transit program (funding shortfalls
following the exhaustion of pandemic-era federal relief funds, and
documented year-over-year increases in bus-operator assaults and reported
disturbances) is a real, recent, concrete illustration that even a
comparatively narrow, single-sector UBS-adjacent policy can lose its
political and fiscal coalition once initial funding conditions change.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — genuine, current, multi-level
political traction (a major party's leadership, a sustained G7-adjacent
policy-research presence, and strong rising public support for the
underlying principle) weighed against the absence of polling on the
comprehensive branded package itself and a real, recent, single-sector
reversal illustrating the durability risk once initial funding conditions
shift.

#### C5.5 Cultural Adaptability: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The seven-sector framework has been explicitly aimed at
multiple national contexts through the Think7/G7 policy-engagement channel
(not a UK-only proposal by design), and its constituent evidence base spans
several distinct wealthy and middle-income cultural contexts: the UK
(the original costing), Austria (Vienna's housing precedent), Canada
(Quebec's childcare precedent, later nationalized), and two small European
states (Luxembourg and Estonia, for transport). This is a real, if
geographically concentrated, spread. Against a stronger score: every one
of these contexts is a high-income, Western or Western-adjacent
democracy; this evaluation located no genuine low-income-country
implementation or adaptation of the comprehensive concept comparable to
Mutual Credit/LETS's own Kenyan example, and no explicit parameter-
adjustment guidance for different income contexts comparable to CCO-PTF's
own explicit high/middle/low-income parameters.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — genuine, multi-country evidence
concentrated in wealthy contexts, with no low-income-country validation
and no explicit cross-context adaptation guidance, matching the pattern
already established for Georgism's own C5.5 score.

**Domain 5 Total: 3.5/5 (70%)** — Two clean passes (C5.1 and C5.3, both
strongly evidenced), no failures, three partials — this is UBS's strongest
domain by a clear margin, and, among this corpus's targeted-mechanism
systems scored under the v2 structure so far, second only to Mutual
Credit/LETS's own 4.0/5 here.

## Final Assessment

**Key Strengths:**
- **C5.1 and C5.3** (Proven Component Foundation and Partial/Parallel
  Deployability) are both clean Passes on the strength of an unusually
  broad, diverse, and — in several cases — multi-decade or multi-generational
  real-world component base spanning at least five distinct service
  sectors (health, education, housing, childcare, transport), each
  independently and repeatedly adopted by different countries for
  different reasons. Domain 5 overall (3.5/5, 70%) is UBS's strongest
  domain and one of the stronger Implementation Viability scores in this
  corpus.
- **C3.4** (Epistemic Adaptability) benefits from an unusually well-
  documented, near-decade-long record of the framework's own core authors
  (the IGP/Coote/Gough lineage) revising scope and framing in response to
  critique and changing context, continuing through sustained engagement
  with the G7's own Think7 policy process into the 2026 cycle.
- **C4.5** (Exploitation Elimination) and, more narrowly, **C1.3** (Housing
  Security) both draw genuine strength from the shelter sector's specific,
  costed commitment — a real mechanism that, for the households it
  reaches, achieves a cleaner elimination of landlord-tenant extraction
  than several other systems' own housing-relevant mechanisms manage.
- **C4.2** (Ecological Compliance) is anchored by directly quantified,
  causally-designed evidence (Luxembourg's measured ~5.9% road-transport
  emissions reduction from its 2020 fare-free transit policy) rather than
  argument alone, and by a current, 106-country peer-reviewed academic
  finding (Vogel et al., 2021) connecting public-service quality generally
  to lower energy requirements for meeting human needs.

**Key Deficiencies:**
- All three structural failures (C1.2a, C1.2b, C1.5) trace to one root
  cause, disclosed explicitly during this evaluation's own drafting
  process: UBS's mechanisms — most concretely its shelter sector — reduce
  what people must spend, but never capture, cap, or redistribute any
  existing stock of wealth, and never give any participant an asset of
  their own to accumulate. C1.2b in particular was revised down from an
  initial, more generous read during drafting (see that criterion's own
  Rationale) upon recognizing that providing a new free service and
  capturing an existing wealth stream are not the same kind of mechanism,
  however similar they may look in a housing-policy summary.
- **C4.3's racial and gender equity finding is flagged as this
  evaluation's second most contestable score**, resting on a genuinely
  strong, quantified universalist case (the Stuber and Schlesinger stigma
  finding; Quebec's gender-pay-gap effect) weighed against the standard
  corpus-wide absence of an explicit racial-reparative mechanism and a
  real, documented rollout-equity concern in the closest real-world
  precedent.
- **C2.2's score reveals a subtlety easy to miss in casual treatments of
  UBS**: the proposal's own "universal" branding means universally
  *available without stigma to whoever needs it*, not "everyone receives
  the same thing regardless of need" — a real, substantive difference from
  a flatly unconditional baseline (UBI, CCO-PTF) that costs UBS a full
  Pass on both C1.1-adjacent and C2.2 grounds.
- **No comprehensive, integrated implementation of the seven-sector
  package exists anywhere**; every strength cited under C5.1/C5.3 above is
  evidence for individual *components*, not for the specific, coordinated
  whole this evaluation scores.

**A note on the adequacy-tier result, for the NEEC framework itself.**
Universal Basic Services lands at **13.5/26 (52%)** — an *exact* match,
to the tenth of a point, with Georgism/Land Value Tax's own published
score, independently verified by this session's own script rather than
merely eyeballed. The two systems reach this identical total through
genuinely different profiles: Georgism scores higher on C1.2b (0.5, for
its land-value-capture mechanism — a real point of contact with wealth
*concentration* that this evaluation ultimately concludes UBS's own
housing plank does not share) while UBS scores higher on C5.1 (1.0, for
its broader and more diversely-proven component base against Georgism's
own component record, which is genuinely strong but pulled down by
Britain's 1910 Land Value Duty as a documented historical integration
failure at comparable intensity). These are the *only* two criteria where
the two systems' scores differ, out of 26 — confirmed programmatically,
not estimated — and, because the totals are exactly equal, **neither
system can possibly formally dominate the other**, a fact guaranteed by
this project's own Theorem 5 (Appendix A.2) the moment the totals matched,
and independently confirmed by direct computation rather than left to
that guarantee alone. Critically, the two systems land in **different
adequacy tiers** despite the exact score tie: Georgism's 2 failures place
it in Potentially Adequate, while UBS's 3rd failure (C1.2b, this
evaluation's most contested call) places it one tier lower, in Partially
Adequate. This is, if anything, a sharper and cleaner illustration than
any this corpus has produced so far of the tier-versus-percentage tension
Section 10.3 already argues is a deliberate design feature rather than
a flaw: two systems can match to the decimal on the scalar total while
the failure-count classification — which the corpus has consistently
argued is the more informative comparative tool — places them a full tier
apart. A future reader weighing whether UBS's C1.2b score should instead
be 0.5 (this evaluation's own disclosed alternative reading) has, in this
exact tie, an unusually clean natural experiment: resolving that single
call would move UBS into Potentially Adequate and produce a second
system tied with Georgism at 14 points, not merely 13.5 — worth flagging
explicitly for whoever next revisits this evaluation or the
Partially-Adequate-tier discussion generally.

**A new comparative "shape," continuing a pattern this project's own
Session 14 handoff explicitly flagged as worth watching for.** Session
14's handoff, closing out the Doughnut Economics evaluation, named two
distinct failure-shapes already visible in this corpus: Georgism and
Mutual Credit/LETS are *narrow, single-mechanism* systems that under-
deliver on breadth; Doughnut Economics is the *opposite* shape, a
comprehensive diagnostic vision that under-delivers on mechanism entirely.
Universal Basic Services is neither of these — it is a **third shape**:
a *comprehensive, multi-sector system that commits to a separate, specific
mechanism for each sector* rather than either one narrow mechanism or no
mechanism at all. This is precisely what the dominance check above makes
visible in miniature: UBS is broadly, though not universally, stronger
than Doughnut Economics (beating it on six criteria: C1.4, C2.1, C2.2,
C4.4, C4.5, and C5.1) while still failing to dominate it outright, since
Doughnut Economics' own ecological criteria (C4.1, C4.2) — where its
planetary-boundaries framing is genuinely more foundational and
definitional than UBS's more recently-added, correlational sufficiency
case — remain its one clear point of superiority. A useful shorthand for
this third shape, offered for whoever next revisits this corpus's
comparative-analysis conventions: UBS is a *federation of committed
mechanisms* — comprehensive in scope like Doughnut Economics, but actually
mechanism-bearing in every sector like Georgism or Mutual Credit/LETS,
rather than mechanism-citing like Doughnut Economics. Whether a future
Ostrom-commons or sovereign-wealth-fund-statism evaluation shares any of
these three now-named shapes is worth checking explicitly rather than
treating each new system's profile as unrelated to what came before.

**Comparison to already-scored systems (qualitative plus three verified
dominance checks, not a full 26-criterion analysis against the entire
corpus).** As with every Step 1b system so far, no exhaustive pairwise
dominance analysis against the complete corpus was attempted in this
pass — that remains queued for a future retrofit-style session, once
Doughnut Economics and Universal Basic Services are both formally
inserted. Three specific comparisons *were* checked programmatically this
session, using the actual canonical `SCORES` dict rather than any
hand-estimated vector: (1) CCO-PTF-CIP-SZH formally, strictly dominates
UBS, as expected given CCO-PTF's near-universal high scores; (2) Georgism
and UBS are confirmed non-dominated, as the exact score tie mathematically
guarantees, differing on exactly C1.2b and C5.1 as detailed above; (3)
Mutual Credit/LETS and UBS are also non-dominated, with Mutual Credit
ahead via C1.2b (its own zero-sum design is a cleaner concentration-
prevention mechanism than UBS's housing plank) and C5.5 (its own
Kenyan low-income-context precedent, which UBS's evidence base lacks),
while UBS is ahead via C1.3 (a real housing-security mechanism against
Mutual Credit/LETS's own flat failure there). Qualitatively, UBS's overall
profile — strong, diverse Implementation Viability; a clean triple-failure
wealth cluster; otherwise consistent partial scores — most closely
resembles Mutual Credit/LETS's own shape among the systems scored so far
(both are Partially Adequate, both have their strongest domain in
Implementation Viability, both fail the wealth cluster cleanly), reached
through entirely different underlying mechanisms (universal in-kind
service provision against peer-to-peer zero-sum credit) — a useful data
point for the same "narrow mechanisms tend to share this shape" pattern
Doughnut Economics' own evaluation first raised, now visible for a second,
broader-scope system as well.
