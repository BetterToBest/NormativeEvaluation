Key findings

  • 23 systems on 29 criteria: 8 Potentially Adequate, 7 Partially Adequate, 8 Structurally Inadequate. Tiers depend only on the number of structural failures, so they do not move with the weights.
  • Among the configured national economies, existing or historical, the highest total is Nordic Social Democracy's: 15.5 of 29 (53%), with 0 structural failures, Potentially Adequate.
  • A higher total can sit in a lower tier: Ostrom Commons (15.0, 3 failures) outscores Degrowth Economics (14.0, 1 failures) and sits one tier lower.
  • No system scores 1.0 on 13 of the 29 criteria: C1.1, C1.2b, C1.4, C2.1, C2.3, C2.4, C2.6, C3.1, C3.3, C3.5, C4.2, C4.3, C4.6.
  • The most often failed criterion is C2.2 Labor Non-Necessity: 11 of 23 systems score 0.0 on it.
  • 17 systems are not dominated: no other system scores at least as high on every criterion and higher on one. Understanding the frontier and dominance

One system dominates another when it scores at least as high on every criterion and higher on at least one. The dominated system then has a rival that is no worse anywhere and better somewhere, under the criteria as written.

The Pareto frontier is the set of systems that no other system dominates. On the chart it is drawn for the two dimensions you choose: frontier systems are ringed in green, labelled, and joined by the dashed line. It depends on the axes, so a system can sit on the frontier for one pair of dimensions and off it for another. The statement that matters most is the one across every criterion at once.

Across all 29 criteria, 17 of the 23 systems are on the frontier: CCO-PTF-PTH-SZH-CIP, Market Socialism, Nordic Social Democracy, Participatory Economics, Integral, Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism, Georgism / LVT, Ostrom Commons, Islamic Finance, Universal Basic Income, Mutual Credit / LETS, State Capitalism / Singapore, Status Quo Market Capitalism, Fully Automated Luxury Communism, State Capitalism / China, State Capitalism / Qatar, Centrally Planned Socialism. Each of the other 6 is dominated by at least one of them. The number after each name is how many criteria it scores higher on; on every other criterion the two score the same.

Dominance is the strongest comparison the scores support, because it needs no weights: under the criteria as written, preferring a dominated system to one that dominates it gives up points somewhere and gains none anywhere. It says nothing about what the criteria leave out, and nothing about margins: one system can dominate another by a single half point on a single criterion. A system off the frontier is not thereby a poor one, and a system on it can still carry structural failures; its tier says how many. The comparison tool sets any two or three systems side by side, criterion by criterion.
