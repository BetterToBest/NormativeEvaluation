## 21 [pending renumbering]. State Capitalism / Qatar

### Overview

**What is scored.** Paper Section 8.1 defines this sub-entry as
rentier-distributive statism: citizen welfare funded mainly by resource
rents flowing through sovereign wealth funds and direct state employment,
rather than by productive-sector industrial policy (China, Singapore) or by
tax-and-transfer redistribution (Nordic Social Democracy). Section 8.1 left
open whether to score a composite of the UAE, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia or a
single representative jurisdiction. This evaluation scores the State of
Qatar's political economy as configured at the evaluation date (September
2026), informed by its longer record. The configured-system scope logic of
the China and Singapore entries is reused.

**Scope decisions (recommended in Handoff 20; confirmed by the user in
Session 21).**

- **A single jurisdiction, not a composite.** A composite would average
  different mechanisms, which Appendix H.6 warns against, and would need
  weighting choices a second scorer could not reproduce. China and
  Singapore were each scored as one national political economy.
- **Qatar rather than the other candidates.** Qatar's public finances match
  the Section 8.1 definition closely: oil and gas supply QAR 155 billion of
  the QAR 199 billion in budgeted 2026 revenue, 77.9% (computed from the
  Government Communications Office's budget statement), and more than 75%
  of employed Qataris worked in government in 2018 (Planning and Statistics
  Authority survey, as reported by Doha News). The alternatives fit less
  well:
  - **UAE.** A federation whose largest sovereign funds belong to one
    emirate: Global SWF lists ADIA, Mubadala, and ADQ as Abu Dhabi's
    (2025 GSR Scoreboard summary). A federal score would blend Abu Dhabi's
    rentier core with the more diversified emirates. This is a structural
    argument; the other emirates' economies were not researched.
  - **Saudi Arabia.** The Public Investment Fund's 2026–2030 strategy
    centres on building domestic economic ecosystems (PIF, April 2026),
    which is closer to the industrial-policy logic Section 8.1 assigns to
    China and Singapore.
  - **Kuwait.** The rentier-state literature's classic case, but not one of
    the three states Section 8.1 names, and its emir dissolved parliament
    and suspended constitutional articles for up to four years in May 2024
    (The National; Library of Congress).
- **Population scope: everyone who lives and works in Qatar, non-citizens
  included.** This follows the Singapore precedent: the migrant workforce is
  a designed, load-bearing feature of the configuration, not an incidental
  population. The citizens-only reading is scored as a scenario in Summary
  Scores. It matters far more here than for Singapore, and it is the only
  choice in this evaluation that can move the tier.
- **The 2026 war is in scope as evidence.** The evaluation date falls six
  months into the regional war that began with US and Israeli strikes on
  Iran on 28 February 2026, after which Iran struck all six Gulf states
  (Middle East Council on Global Affairs). The war is not part of the
  system's design, but how the configuration absorbed it is direct evidence
  for C1.3, C1.4, C3.1, and C3.3.
- **Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism and the two siblings stay separate.**
  Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism scored the fund mechanism alone (Norway and
  Alaska) and set the Gulf funds aside by design. It is used here as a
  comparator.

**The system's shape.** Four features carry most of the load:

- **Hydrocarbon rents through the state.** State-owned QatarEnergy operates
  an LNG complex with 77 million tonnes a year of capacity and plans to
  reach 142 million by 2030 (Middle East Council on Global Affairs). There
  is no personal income tax (C1.2b). Estimates of the Qatar Investment
  Authority's assets range from about US$526 billion (Heritage Foundation
  figure, as reported by The Peninsula, 2025) to about US$580 billion
  (Global SWF, cited by the Middle East Council on Global Affairs, 2026);
  both are third-party estimates.
- **Allocation to citizens.** Free building plots, housing finance at a 1%
  management fee, and a QR 50,000 furniture grant (Qatar Development Bank);
  free basic services within the housing scheme (Ministry of Foreign
  Affairs); income-tested assistance for defined groups (US Social Security
  Administration country profile); a contributory pension for citizens
  (Law No. 1 of 2022); free health care and state schooling (C2.2); and,
  above all, public employment.
- **A majority non-citizen workforce under employer-tied residence.** The
  UN estimates about 3.17 million residents in mid-2026 (via Worldometer).
  Secondary estimates put Qatari nationals at 10.5–11.6% of residents
  (Global Media Insight, 2026, which gives both figures); International
  IDEA says roughly 13% hold citizenship, AFP cites about 380,000 Qatari
  nationals, and Human Rights Watch puts migrant workers at more than 91%
  of the population. This document therefore uses "roughly 9–13%" for the
  citizen share. The 2020 labour laws removed the employer-permission
  requirement for changing jobs and set a non-discriminatory minimum wage
  (ILO); enforcement gaps are documented under C2.1 and C4.5.
- **A hereditary monarchy.** The emir holds all executive and legislative
  authority and political parties are not permitted (Freedom House). A 2024
  referendum ended elections to the advisory Shura Council (International
  IDEA).

**A contested subject, used here as taxonomy only.** Qatar's government and
the ILO describe the reforms since 2018 as dismantling the kafala
sponsorship system, and the Ministry of Labour published a compilation of
international praise for them (Gulf Times, 2022). Human Rights Watch,
Amnesty International, and the US State Department document the system's
practical persistence. The Heritage Foundation rates Qatar "mostly free"
and 27th of 184 economies, while naming reliance on expatriate labour as a
structural limit. This evaluation reports all of these and takes the NEEC
framework as specified. "State capitalism" is Section 8.1's taxonomic term,
not an endorsement of any framing.

**The normative-commitment disclosure.** As with China and Singapore,
several scores below (C2.1, C2.4, C4.3, C4.4) follow partly from NEEC's
stated commitments to freedom from coercion, democratic participation,
equal treatment, and diffused power (Paper Section 2.2). Readers who reject
those commitments should contest them at the level of the criteria, through
Appendix I.

**Evidentiary tier (Appendix H.6): real-world implementation.** The gaps
matter here. No official nationality breakdown of the population was
retrieved; no official poverty line or wealth distribution was found; the
sovereign fund's size is known only from third-party estimates; and the ILO
found that work-injury data could not yield a categorical count of fatal
injuries (C3.5). Where evidence is secondary or absent, the text says so.

**Where this system sits relative to the corpus.** Qatar is the third
instance of the fourth Step 1b archetype, "configured national political
economy" (confirmed in Session 20), after China and Singapore.

### Domain 1: Material Security

#### C1.1 Poverty Elimination Capacity: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Appendix C.1 prescribes a locally costed basic-needs basket
for high-income countries. No official Qatari poverty line was found. The
statistics authority's SDG 1 summary lists at least one poverty indicator as
not applicable to Qatar (Planning and Statistics Authority), and Qatar's
2025 Voluntary National Review has an SDG 1 chapter that this session did
not read. The only internationally comparable figure retrieved is the World
Bank's headcount at its societal poverty line, a line that rises with
median consumption: 17.5% in 2017 (World Bank, via CEIC). That is a
relative measure, and whether the underlying survey reached workers in
labour camps was not checked.

The picture divides by legal status:

- **Citizens.** An income-tested guaranteed minimum income covers older
  people, people with disabilities, widows, divorced women, and some
  orphans and vulnerable families (US Social Security Administration;
  Ministry of Social Development and Family). The housing scheme includes
  housing for needy families covered by social security (Ministry of
  Foreign Affairs), and most employed citizens work for the state (C1.4).
  Deprivation among citizens is plausibly rare, but no citizen poverty
  figure was found.
- **Non-citizens.** The statutory minimum is a basic wage of QAR 1,000
  (about US$274) plus food and housing, or allowances of at least QAR 300
  and QAR 500 in their place (ILO; Human Rights Watch). It has not been
  revised since 2021, and Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International both
  report that it does not cover living costs. Stakeholders interviewed by
  Amnesty named wage theft as the most common form of exploitation (2023
  briefing), and Human Rights Watch traced unpaid wages to late payment by
  clients at the top of contracting chains, government bodies included
  (December 2025). Non-citizens are outside both social assistance and the
  pension system (US Social Security Administration; HLB). The Workers'
  Support and Insurance Fund had paid about QAR 598 million to more than
  36,000 workers by July 2022 (Human Rights Watch, citing the Ministry of
  Labour): a real remedy, but a partial one.

Poverty is also partly exported rather than measured: workers who lose
their jobs usually lose the right to stay and leave (C1.4), so in-country
figures understate the system's failures.

No documented poverty-reduction rate exists to compare with the 90% Pass
Threshold, so this score rests on reasoned estimation (H.6). The continuous
estimate is about 0.35–0.55: deprivation is rare among citizens, but most
residents depend on a wage floor that monitors describe as inadequate and
on enforcement that repeatedly fails.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5). **Flagged as contestable:**
measured against an extreme-poverty line, the reading behind China's and
Singapore's flagged alternatives and the Centrally Planned Socialism
precedent, mandatory employer-provided food and housing would put measured
extreme poverty close to zero. This evaluation follows Appendix C.1's
high-income rule, as Singapore's did. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 10
failures; tier unchanged. Citizens-only scenario: 1.0.

#### C1.2a Wealth Building for Resilience: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** For citizens, this is one of the strongest accumulation
mechanisms in the Step 1b cohort. The state allocates free building plots
and finances homes at a 1% management fee instead of interest, with a QR
50,000 furniture grant (Qatar Development Bank; Ministry of Foreign
Affairs). The housing loan was reported at QR 600,000 plus a further QR
600,000 from the Council of Ministers (The Peninsula, 2015). Public-sector
pay has been reported at almost three times private-sector pay (a Labour
Force Sample Survey reported by Gulf News; the survey year is not given),
and a contributory pension covers citizens in public and private employment
(Law No. 1 of 2022, per HLB). Naturalised citizens qualify for the housing
loan only after fifteen years of citizenship (The Peninsula, citing Cabinet
Resolution No. 3 of 2008).

For most residents, the mechanism does not exist:

- **No pension or social insurance.** The pension system covers citizens
  (and some GCC nationals) and excludes foreign workers (HLB; US Social
  Security Administration). Private-sector workers receive an
  end-of-service gratuity under Labour Law No. 14 of 2004 (Commoner Law
  summary), the only statutory accumulation device found for them.
- **Restricted property ownership.** Foreigners may buy only in designated
  freehold and leasehold developments, and central-bank rules cap an
  expatriate borrower's mortgage payments at half of salary (Expatica).
- **Debt at entry.** Recruitment fees of US$1,000–3,000 leave many workers
  needing months or years to repay (Amnesty International, 2022).
- **The arithmetic.** Accumulating $60,000 over twenty years takes $250 a
  month, about 91% of the US$274 basic minimum wage (computed). A worker on
  the minimum basic wage would have to save almost all of it for twenty
  years.

Counting citizens (roughly 9–13% of residents) and the higher-paid
professionals who have private options, this evaluation estimates that well
under half of residents have a genuine mechanism clearing the bar, far
below the threshold's 70% (reasoning, not a citable figure).

**A conflicting figure, disclosed.** UBS figures reported by Yahoo Finance
and lovemoney (2026) put Qatar's median wealth per adult at $92,789, and the
latest UBS ranking as reproduced on Wikipedia gives $95,499, almost level
with Singapore's $96,434. Taken at face value, that clears the Pass
Threshold. This evaluation could not reconcile it with the documented wage
structure of a population in which non-citizens are the large majority, and
UBS's estimation method for Qatar was not retrieved. No score rests on it.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a genuine and generous mechanism,
but a narrow one, closed to most residents (the H.7v2 0.5 band). This
differs from Singapore's 1.0, whose mechanism reached about three-quarters
of the population. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader who accepts the UBS
median would score 1.0. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 10 failures; tier
unchanged. Citizens-only scenario: 1.0.

#### C1.2b Prevention of Exploitative Accumulation: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** No official wealth-distribution figure for Qatar was found.
Commentary on UBS's figures says Qatar has the second-best wealth Gini among
the 25 countries with the highest median wealth (Yahoo Finance and
lovemoney); the underlying number was not retrieved. The allocation
described under C1.2a is a real deconcentrating channel: it spreads
rent-derived land and housing wealth across the citizen body rather than
leaving it to market allocation.

Three considerations cut the other way:

- **Nothing limits concentration at the top.** There is no personal income
  tax (The Peninsula, summarizing the Heritage Foundation's 2025 index,
  which scores Qatar's tax burden at 99.9 of 100 according to Qatar
  Living), and no wealth or inheritance tax was found. How resource rents
  are divided between the state and the ruling family was not researched in
  this session.
- **Fund governance is middling.** Global SWF scores the Qatar Investment
  Authority at 5 of 10 on governance (GSR Scoreboard 2026).
- **The channel is closed to most residents,** so the distribution across
  all residents very likely sits far above the 0.35 threshold (reasoning;
  no figure exists).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a real deconcentrating mechanism
for citizens, with no structural cap and no confirmation of a wealth Gini
below 0.35. This matches Nordic Social Democracy, Sovereign Wealth Fund
Statism, China, and Singapore. **Flagged as contestable:** under the
confirmed all-resident scope, a reader could treat the citizenship divide
and the absence of any limit at the top as the H.7v2 0.0 case. Consequence,
checked: 8.5/26 with 11 failures; tier unchanged.

#### C1.3 Housing Security: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** For citizens, housing is largely decommodified. The Housing
Law (No. 2 of 2007) provides free building plots, long-term housing loans
for those able to repay, housing for needy families covered by social
security, free basic services such as electricity and water, and
maintenance of homes for elderly citizens (Ministry of Foreign Affairs).
Loans of up to QR 1.2 million for rebuilding, and grants of up to QR 1
million for demolition and reconstruction, are also available (ILoveQatar
guide). By the anchor's standard, this is a 1.0-type mechanism for
citizens.

For non-citizens, housing is tied to employment. The minimum-wage law
requires employers to provide decent accommodation or a housing allowance
of at least QAR 500 (ILO), so a mechanism exists. But a worker's residence
permit depends on the employer. Employers can cancel visas or file
absconding charges, leaving workers undocumented (US State Department,
*Trafficking in Persons Report 2025*). During the 2026 war, a contractor at
Ras Laffan dismissed a maintenance worker and handed him a flight ticket
only three hours before departure (Business & Human Rights Resource Centre,
reporting a June 2026 investigation). Five-year housing stability for most
residents therefore depends on continuous employment with a cooperating
employer. Foreigners may buy homes only in designated developments
(Expatica).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — full decommodification for the
minority and a real but employment-contingent mechanism for the majority
(the H.7 0.5 band). Citizens-only scenario: 1.0.

#### C1.4 Automation Resilience: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The threshold asks whether poverty stays under 8% and demand
above 85% of baseline under 30%, 50%, and 70% job displacement.

The configuration separates citizens' incomes from market labour to an
unusual degree. Oil and gas supply most budgeted revenue (Scope decisions:
77.9% in 2026), and there is no personal income tax. More than 75% of
employed Qataris worked in government in 2018, while the private sector
employed 79% of the labour force (Planning and Statistics Authority, as
reported by Doha News). Automating the largely non-citizen private
workforce would not directly erode either citizens' incomes or the state's
revenue. But citizens' main income channel is still a job, a state job, and
no income guarantee independent of employment was found (C2.2).

For non-citizens, the system adjusts to job loss by removal rather than
support. They are outside social assistance and pensions (C1.1), and their
residence depends on employment (C1.3). Gulf migration research notes that
earlier crises reduced migrant populations selectively, through layoffs,
non-renewal, and departures, with low-wage workers most exposed (Gulf
Research Center commentary, 2026). Displaced workers leave the population
the criterion measures; that is externalization, not resilience.

The 2026 war also showed that the rent base itself can be hit. Iranian
strikes in March damaged two of Qatar's fourteen LNG trains, removing 12.8
million tonnes a year of capacity for three to five years, and QatarEnergy's
chief executive put the revenue loss at about US$20 billion a year (Al
Jazeera, citing Reuters). That is 12.8 of 77 million tonnes a year, 16.6%
(computed), consistent with the roughly 17% reported. Qatar recorded a
first-quarter 2026 budget deficit of QAR 10.3 billion as oil and gas
revenue fell from QAR 42.5 billion to QAR 32.7 billion, a 23.1% fall
(computed; Reuters, via Zawya). JPMorgan estimated that GDP could contract
by about 9% in 2026 (as reported by Roic News).

The continuous estimate is about 0.35–0.6: a real buffer for the citizen
minority, funded by a rent stream that automation would not touch, and no
mechanism for the majority beyond departure.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a buffer exists, but it is not
designed for the higher-severity scenarios for most of the population. This
matches Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism, China, and Singapore.

#### C1.5 Universal Wealth Access (narrowed — access breadth only): 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** The narrowed threshold asks whether at least 80% of the
population has an active wealth-accumulation pathway. Under the confirmed
scope, the pathways described under C1.2a reach citizens and a minority of
higher-paid foreign residents. The route into the citizen group is closed
for practical purposes:

- **Permanent residency** is capped at 100 new permits a year (Library of
  Congress, on Law No. 10 of 2018). Permanent residents receive state
  health care and schooling (Library of Congress) and, according to Al
  Jazeera's 2018 report, the same social benefits as citizens. A separate
  route grants permanent residency to buyers of property worth QAR 3.65
  million or more (Commoner Law; Wego).
- **Naturalisation** requires 25 years of continuous residence, is granted
  by emiri decree, and is described as exceedingly rare; foreign husbands
  of Qatari women have no route through marriage (Commoner Law). Qatari
  women cannot pass their nationality to their children or foreign spouses
  (Amnesty International).

This evaluation estimates that roughly 20–35% of residents have an active
pathway: citizens, permanent residents and property investors, and
professionals with private options (reasoning, not a citable figure). The
excluded group is not a segment but the majority, and membership is
acquired almost entirely by birth.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — access is concentrated
among those already positioned to have it, by descent, and the excluded
group is most of the population (the H.7v2 0.0 band). This differs from
Singapore's 0.5, where the excluded group was about a fifth of the
population. **Flagged as contestable:** the Market Socialism anchor treats
a membership gate as the 0.5 case, and a reader could read citizenship the
same way. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 9 failures; tier unchanged.
Citizens-only scenario: 1.0.

**Domain 1 = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.0 = 2.5/6 (42%)**

### Domain 2: Human Autonomy

#### C2.1 Freedom from Coercion: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Most residents live under an employer-tied residence system.
In the ILO's assessment, the August 2020 law ending the requirement for an
employer's No Objection Certificate to change jobs, together with the
removal of exit permits earlier that year, dismantled the core of the
kafala sponsorship system (ILO, 2020). The Ministry of Labour approved more
than 350,000 job-change applications in the reforms' first two years
(ILO).

Monitors report that coercion persists in practice:

- Leaving an employer without permission is still criminalised as
  "absconding," even when a worker is escaping abuse, and passport
  confiscation and illegal recruitment fees remain common and largely
  unpunished (Human Rights Watch, *World Report 2026*).
- Officials reportedly still require a release letter from the current
  employer before a transfer, and employers retaliate against transfers by
  cancelling visas or filing absconding charges (US State Department,
  *Trafficking in Persons Report 2025*).
- Workers still depend on employers to enter and stay in Qatar, and they
  are barred from organising (Amnesty International UK, January 2026).
- Freedom House scores Qatar 1 of 4 on freedom to change residence and
  employment, and 1 of 4 on freedom from economic exploitation (*Freedom
  in the World 2026*).

For non-citizens there is no safety net: losing a job means losing income
and, usually, the right to stay (C1.3, C2.2). Survival compulsion is
therefore at its maximum, by design.

Citizens face the reverse profile: little economic compulsion, but tight
political constraints (Freedom House scores political rights at 7 of 40)
and male-guardianship rules. Human Rights Watch reports that women need a
guardian's permission to marry, to take many government jobs, to receive
some reproductive health care, and, if unmarried and under 25, to travel
abroad (World Reports 2023 and 2024); the government disputes parts of that
account (Human Rights Watch, 2021).

The continuous estimate is about 0.15–0.35, straddling the rounding
boundary: the reforms are real, but for the majority the design still
concentrates coercive power in employers.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — economic coercion is
maximized by design for most residents (the H.7 0.0 anchor). This differs
from Singapore's 0.5, whose permit regime covered about a fifth of the
population. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader weighting the 2020
reforms and the 350,000 approved job changes would score 0.5, the band for
a system that meaningfully reduces coercion relative to a harsher baseline.
Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 9 failures; tier unchanged.
Citizens-only scenario: 0.5.

#### C2.2 Labor Non-Necessity: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Non-citizens have no baseline independent of work. Their
residence depends on employment, and they are excluded from social
assistance and the pension system (US Social Security Administration;
HLB). Citizens have more: free land and housing finance (C1.3), free health
care and state schooling, which the 2018 permanent-residency law extended
to permanent residents (Al Jazeera), and an income-tested minimum income
for specified groups (US Social Security Administration). But the citizen
baseline is conditional: assistance is means-tested and limited to defined
categories, and the main income channel is public employment.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — for most of the
population, presence and survival depend on labour-market participation.
This matches China and Singapore. Citizens-only scenario: 0.5, a real but
conditional baseline (the Nordic anchor case).

#### C2.3 Creative Development Opportunities: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The evidence for this criterion is thinner than for most.
Constraints are documented: Freedom House scores media independence at 1
of 4, and academic freedom and freedom of personal expression at 2 of 4
each (2026). For low-wage workers, long hours limit non-subsistence time;
one Bangladeshi café worker in Doha described 12- to 14-hour days (Business
& Human Rights Resource Centre, 2026), an anecdote rather than a statistic.
For citizens, public-sector employment has been associated with shorter
hours and longer holidays (Gulf News, reporting the labour force survey).
State investment in culture and education was not researched in this
session, and no data on creative engagement were found.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — engagement is constrained by
working time for most residents and by limits on expression, without
evidence of the comprehensive state control of cultural production that the
0.0 anchor describes. This matches China and Singapore, on less direct
evidence than either had.

#### C2.4 Democratic Participation: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Qatar's hereditary emir holds all executive and legislative
authority and ultimately controls the judiciary; political parties are not
permitted; and most residents are non-citizens without political rights
(Freedom House). The only national legislative election, in 2021, filled 30
of the advisory Shura Council's 45 seats. Eligibility was limited to
descendants of families who were citizens in 1930, which excluded
naturalised citizens and some members of the Al Murrah tribe (AFP, via Gulf
News; VOA). In November 2024, citizens approved constitutional amendments,
with 84% turnout and 90.6% in favour, that ended those elections and
returned the council to full appointment (International IDEA). Municipal
council elections have been held every four years since 1999 (AFP); the
council's powers were not researched in this session. Freedom House scores
every electoral-process and political-pluralism indicator at 0 or 1 of 4
(2026).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — no meaningful
participation mechanism for the population as a whole, and a narrowing one
for citizens. This matches China and the Centrally Planned Socialism
precedent.

#### C2.5 Exit Rights and Mobility: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Formal exit has improved: migrant workers no longer need an
exit permit to leave the country (ILO). Qatar's funding also does not
depend on near-universal participation, since most revenue comes from
hydrocarbons (C1.4), so the Nordic-style structural failure does not apply.

Real costs remain. Changing employers is obstructed in practice (C2.1), and
recruitment debt and withheld wages make leaving expensive (C1.2a, C1.1).
Unmarried Qatari women under 25 need a guardian's permission to travel
abroad, and men can petition courts to bar female relatives from travelling
(Human Rights Watch, *World Report 2023*). Penal code amendments enacted in
October 2024 set prison terms of up to three years for illegally entering
or leaving the country (Freedom House, 2025). Freedom House scores freedom
of movement, including changes of residence and employment, at 1 of 4.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — formal exit rights, constrained by
real costs that the configuration itself creates for most residents. This
matches China, Singapore, and the Status Quo anchor.

**Domain 2 = 0.0 + 0.0 + 0.5 + 0.0 + 0.5 = 1.0/5 (20%)**

### Domain 3: System Resilience

#### C3.1 Crisis Response Capacity: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Qatar's crisis responses have been fast and large, and they
face no legislative delay, because the emir holds legislative authority
(Freedom House). After the 2017 blockade, the state opened Hamad Port
within six months and used it to secure new supply routes (Al Jazeera,
2020), and it built a strategic food reserve sized for six months
(*Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems*, 2025). The IMF judged the economy
resilient in 2019 (as reported by Al Jazeera). From March 2020, the state
deployed a QR 75 billion stimulus package and suspended customs duties on
food and medical goods for six months (Qatar Chamber, citing the Qatar News
Agency). The buffers are deep: the sovereign fund (Overview) and about
US$72 billion in central-bank reserves (Middle East Council on Global
Affairs, 2026).

Three limits keep this below a Pass:

- **Responses are discretionary, not automatic.**
- **Coverage is uneven.** Non-citizens are outside social assistance and
  pensions, and Human Rights Watch judges the Wage Protection System and
  the wage support fund insufficient to prevent or remedy wage theft
  (December 2025).
- **The 2026 record shows restraint rather than expansion.**
  First-quarter 2026 spending fell 3.7% year on year (Reuters, via Zawya),
  and migrant workers were dismissed and sent home (C1.3). No
  Qatar-specific 2026 support package for residents was found in this
  session's sources; the absence is noted, not asserted as fact. In
  neighbouring Bahrain, migrant workers were excluded from an emergency
  wage-support program (Human Rights Watch, June 2026).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a fast, well-funded,
discretionary response with incomplete coverage. This matches Sovereign
Wealth Fund Statism, China, and Singapore.

#### C3.2 Inflation Control Mechanisms: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** The riyal is pegged to the US dollar, and the Qatar Central
Bank sets its policy rates in close alignment with the Federal Reserve's
(IMF, 2025). Consumer-price inflation averaged 3.2% a year from 1980 to
2024 and peaked at 15.1% in 2008, before turning negative in 2009 (IMF data,
via TheGlobalEconomy.com); monthly inflation peaked at 16.59% in June 2008
(National Planning Council data, via Trading Economics). Recent inflation
is low: 1.3% in 2024 (IMF) and 2.51% in February 2026 (Trading Economics).
The long-run average sits just above the 3% threshold, and the 2008 peak
was about three times the 5% stress bound (computed). Because a peg imports
US monetary policy, it cannot tighten against a purely domestic boom (a
structural observation, not a sourced finding about 2008).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a conventional mechanism without
novel safeguards, with a documented stress-test breach (the band of the
H.7 MMT anchor). This differs from China's and Singapore's 1.0, which
rested on distinctive mechanisms.

#### C3.3 Multi-Failure Resistance: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Qatar has absorbed three compound shocks in under a decade:

- **2017–2021.** The blockade by Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt
  closed Qatar's only land border and airspace routes (Tajseer journal,
  2023) and cut off suppliers of almost 30% of its food imports (Springer
  chapter, 2023), in the aftermath of the 2014–2016 fall in hydrocarbon
  prices (IMF assessment, as reported by Al Jazeera).
- **2020.** The pandemic, an oil-price collapse, and the continuing
  blockade at once; GDP contracted by an estimated 2.5% (IMF estimate, as
  reported by *The Business Year*, 2021).
- **2026.** War, strikes on Ras Laffan and on Hamad International Airport,
  force majeure at the fertiliser producer QAFCO, and blocked passage
  through the Strait of Hormuz (Middle East Council on Global Affairs);
  JPMorgan's estimated GDP contraction is about 9% (C1.4).

Measured by output, degradation stayed below the threshold's 20% in each
episode, though the 2026 figure is still an estimate. Financial redundancy
and the food reserves built after the blockade did real work. But the 2026
war exposed structural concentration. Ras Laffan concentrates all of
Qatar's LNG liquefaction and export docks, together with power generation
and desalination, within about ten kilometres, and LNG has no export route
that avoids Hormuz (Middle East Council on Global Affairs). The same
analysis notes that the blockade-era buffers did not protect against
strikes on production.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — real financial and supply
redundancy, around a single-site, single-chokepoint, single-commodity core
that transmits shocks. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader weighting three
absorbed compound shocks, each with output losses below 20%, would score
1.0. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 10 failures; tier unchanged.

#### C3.4 Epistemic Adaptability: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Qatar has changed course quickly more than once. After the
2014–2016 fall in hydrocarbon prices, it cut current spending and began
energy-pricing and labour reforms (IMF, 2017). The blockade pushed it
toward self-reliance, industrialisation, and easier foreign investment
(Springer chapter). The labour reforms of 2018–2021 were designed with the
ILO. The Third National Development Strategy (2024–2030) calls for a
decisive shift from a costly state-driven growth model to a
private-sector-led one (IMF, 2025).

The limits are documented as well. The threshold asks for democratic
governance of changes, and Qatar has none (C2.4). Several adjustments
followed external pressure and left implementation gaps (ILO; Human Rights
Watch). The minimum wage has not been revised since 2021 despite a standing
Minimum Wage Commission (ILO; Human Rights Watch, 2026). A value-added tax,
which IMF staff recommended again in 2026, has still not been introduced
(IMF; *Global Business Outlook*, 2026). And the 2024 constitutional
amendments reversed the country's only experiment with national elections.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — adaptation that is real and
sometimes rapid, but top-down, often externally prompted, and slow where
interests resist. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader weighting the speed
of the post-2014 and post-blockade adjustments would score 1.0, as
Singapore and Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism were scored. Consequence,
checked: 9.5/26 with 10 failures; tier unchanged.

#### C3.5 Failure-Mode Transparency: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** The record points in both directions.

- **Visibility.** Qatar invited the ILO in. The resulting report, *One is
  too many* (2021), drew on all medical institutions providing acute care
  to injured workers and counted at least 50 work-related deaths and 506
  severe injuries in 2020 (ILO; AFP). A peer-reviewed description of the
  national injury registry found 2020 rates lower than or comparable to
  those of other high-income countries (PubMed Central). The finance
  ministry publishes quarterly budget results (Reuters, via Zawya).
- **Gaps and suppression.** The same ILO report said no categorical count
  of fatal occupational injuries was possible, and the ILO said the
  authorities do not classify unexplained deaths of healthy workers or
  heat-related deaths as work-related (AFP, via Malay Mail). Qatar has
  never given precise figures for deaths of foreign workers (AFP, 2022),
  and Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International report that the
  authorities failed to investigate thousands of deaths. The UN Working
  Group on Arbitrary Detention found that a former World Cup organising
  committee employee was arbitrarily detained after raising concerns about
  migrant workers (Freedom House, 2025). Freedom House scores government
  openness and media independence at 1 of 4 each, and freedom for
  human-rights organisations at 0 of 4 (2026). Global SWF scores the
  sovereign fund's governance at 5 of 10.
- **Externalization.** Major costs fall outside the system's own accounts:
  recruitment debt borne by workers, uncompensated deaths, and the world's
  highest per-capita emissions (C4.2).
- **For balance.** The ILO also cautioned that a widely reported estimate
  of 6,500 migrant deaths counted all deaths in the migrant population, not
  only work-related ones (AFP, via Malay Mail).

The continuous estimate is about 0.2–0.35, straddling the rounding
boundary.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — deaths left
unexplained, and a worker who raised concerns detained: this goes beyond
Singapore's gaps in measurement and follows China's 0.0. **Flagged as
contestable:** a reader weighting the ILO cooperation, the injury registry,
and the published fiscal data would score 0.5. Consequence, checked:
9.5/26 with 9 failures; tier unchanged.

### Domain 4: Ethical Integrity

#### C4.1 Intergenerational Justice: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Qatar saves a large share of its resource wealth. Estimates
of the sovereign fund's assets range from about US$526 billion to about
US$580 billion (Overview). IMF staff judged in 2026 that the fiscal stance
is consistent with intergenerational equity, and general government debt
was about 40–42% of GDP (an IMF estimate for 2025, as cited in an April
2026 Qatar economic report; the Heritage Foundation, 2025), far below the
threshold's 80%.

The negative transfer is also large. Qatar has the world's highest
per-capita CO₂ emissions (C4.2); its fossil CO₂ emissions rose 4.7% in
2024 (EDGAR data, via Worldometer); and its growth plan nearly doubles LNG
capacity by 2030 (Overview). The gas behind the fund is a depleting
reserve. Converting it into financial assets is the intergenerational case
for the fund, but the emissions from producing and burning the gas are not
offset.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — a large positive financial
transfer alongside a large negative climate transfer: the shape of the
Nordic anchor, at a larger scale. Like China and Singapore, Qatar is scored
as a whole economy. **Flagged as contestable:** Sovereign Wealth Fund
Statism scored 1.0 on the strength of the fund's founding purpose, and a
reader applying that precedent here would score 1.0. That evaluation scored
a fund mechanism layered onto a market economy, not a whole national
configuration. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 10 failures; tier
unchanged.

#### C4.2 Ecological Compliance: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Qatar had the highest per-capita CO₂ emissions in the world
in 2023, at 42.6 tonnes (Statista). EDGAR data put its fossil CO₂ emissions
at about 135 million tonnes in 2024 (Worldometer). The energy sector
accounts for more than 96% of emissions, and Qatar has no comprehensive
carbon-pricing mechanism (Middle East Council on Global Affairs, 2025). Its
NDC pledges a 25% cut against a business-as-usual scenario that it does not
publish; Climate Analytics estimates the implied 2030 level at 147–213
MtCO₂e and says a 1.5°C-compatible path would require a 58% cut below 2015
levels by 2030. The LNG expansion is the core growth strategy.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — growth-dependent, with
a relative rather than absolute target (the H.7 0.0 anchor). This matches
Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism, China, and Singapore. Unlike Singapore's,
this call is not flagged: there is no carbon price to support a
Nordic-analogy reading.

#### C4.3 Racial and Gender Equity: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Qatar's law distinguishes by nationality rather than treating
residents alike. Rights, benefits, and political participation belong to
citizens (C1.1 to C2.4), so rent-funded benefits flow to the most
advantaged legal group, the reverse of the disproportionate flow to
disadvantaged groups that the 1.0 anchor describes. Freedom House scores
equal treatment of population segments at 0 of 4 (2026). Among citizens,
the 2021 election excluded naturalised citizens and some members of the Al
Murrah tribe (C2.4).

Gender inequality is written into law through male guardianship (C2.1),
and Qatari women cannot pass their nationality to their children or
foreign spouses (Amnesty International). Reforms exist: since January
2020, women can obtain driving licences without a guardian's permission
(Human Rights Watch, 2021), and the 2021 minimum wage applies to workers of
all nationalities (ILO).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — formal differentiation,
not formal neutrality, amid large disparities and with no reparative
mechanism. The known inconsistency in the H.7 anchor for this criterion
(its 0.0 example, Status Quo Market Capitalism, is published at 0.5; Handoff
20) does not affect this call, because Qatar's policy falls below the
formally neutral case under either calibration. This matches China.

#### C4.4 Power Distribution: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Political power is hereditary and concentrated. The emir
holds all executive and legislative authority, parties are not permitted,
and the advisory council is again fully appointed (Freedom House;
International IDEA); no mechanism exists to remove or replace the ruler.
Freedom House scores the question of whether elected officials determine
policy at 0 of 4 (2026). Economic power sits with the same state: the
energy company is state-owned, and the sovereign fund's governance scores 5
of 10 (Global SWF). Anti-corruption enforcement does occur: in January 2024,
a court sentenced a former finance minister to 20 years in prison for
bribery and misuse of public funds, and a member of the ruling family who
was his codefendant to six years (Freedom House, 2025).

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — political and economic
power concentrated in a narrow group. This matches China, and Singapore's
flagged 0.0, without Singapore's electoral counterweight.

#### C4.5 Exploitation Elimination: 0.0 (Structural Failure)

**Rationale:** Extraction from migrant labour is load-bearing. Qataris
were about 5% of the labour force in 2018 (Planning and Statistics
Authority, as reported by Doha News), so the private economy runs on
non-citizen labour. Workers pay recruitment fees (C1.2a); wage theft is the
most common form of exploitation and is sometimes passed down from
government clients (C1.1); workers may not organise (Amnesty International
UK, 2026); and strikes over unpaid wages risk arrest and deportation
(Human Rights Watch, *World Report 2026*). Domestic workers, mostly women,
remain outside the main labour law (Amnesty International). Freedom House
scores freedom from economic exploitation at 1 of 4.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — extraction is a
structural feature of the configuration, not an incidental flaw. This
matches Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism, China, and Singapore. Citizens-only
scenario: 0.5, following Singapore's scope reading, since most citizens
work for the state and their private employment is regulated.

**Domain 4 = 0.5 + 0.0 + 0.0 + 0.0 + 0.0 = 0.5/5 (10%)**

### Domain 5: Implementation Viability

#### C5.1 Proven Component Foundation: 1.0 (Pass)

**Rationale:** The configuration's components have long records at
national scale. Social-assistance law dates from 1963 (current law 1995),
and a citizens' pension system has operated since 2002, replaced by Law
No. 1 of 2022 (US Social Security Administration; HLB). The housing scheme
runs under the 2007 law. Qatar already handled a third of global LNG trade
by 2015 (IMF), and its sovereign fund has operated for about two decades
(the founding date is from background knowledge and was not re-verified in
this session). The citizen body alone numbers about 380,000 (AFP).
Whether the configuration can be reproduced elsewhere is a separate
question (C5.5).

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — decades of operation at national
scale, with documented outcomes. This matches China and Singapore.

#### C5.2 Staged Transition Pathways: 0.5 (Partial)

**Rationale:** Qatar has multi-phase national plans with targets: Qatar
National Vision 2030 and three national development strategies, the third
of which (2024–2030) sets targets and names diversification clusters (IMF,
2025). But these plans develop an existing configuration; they are not a
pathway into it for other polities, and the configuration's precondition,
large resource rents per citizen, cannot be planned into existence (C5.5).
No rapid-deployment plan exists.

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — documented staged planning without
a transferable transition pathway. This matches China and Singapore.

#### C5.3 Partial and Parallel Deployability: 1.0 (Pass) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** The configuration already runs in parallel with a market
economy. Its distributive core serves citizens, roughly 9–13% of residents,
while the private sector employs most of the labour force (C1.4). The
Heritage Foundation ranks Qatar 27th of 184 economies for economic freedom
(Qatar Tribune, 2025). Its best-known component, the sovereign fund, has
been adopted piecemeal in very different settings (Sovereign Wealth Fund
Statism, C5.5).

**Score Justification:** Pass (1.0) — functions at low participation and
coexists with markets. This matches Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism and
Singapore. **Flagged as contestable:** the distributive core is partial by
necessity rather than by design, because rents per beneficiary shrink as
coverage widens, and no validated scaling pathway exists; a reader weighting
that would score 0.5. Consequence, checked: 8.5/26 with 10 failures; tier
unchanged.

#### C5.4 Political Coalition Potential: 0.5 (Partial) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** Among citizens, the distributive bargain appears durable: in
the 2024 referendum, 84% of eligible citizens voted and 90.6% of valid votes
approved the amendments (International IDEA). But support across a
political spectrum cannot be observed where parties are banned, the media
is not independent, and most residents have no political rights (Freedom
House). Outside Qatar, the configuration draws criticism from several
directions: human-rights monitors on labour and guardianship, climate
analysts on emissions, and the Heritage Foundation on government spending
and reliance on expatriate labour (The Peninsula, 2025). The IMF, for its
part, credits sound macroeconomic management (2026).

**Score Justification:** Partial (0.5) — broad acquiescence among the
enfranchised minority, with no observable cross-spectrum support.
**Flagged as contestable:** a reader treating the referendum and the
bargain's durability as cross-constituency support would score 1.0, as
Singapore's flag did for six decades of majorities. Consequence, checked:
9.5/26 with 10 failures; tier unchanged. A downward reading (0.0, as in
China's flag) is also arguable; it would add a failure but, with ten
already, could not change the tier.

#### C5.5 Cultural Adaptability: 0.0 (Structural Failure) — flagged as contestable

**Rationale:** The rentier-distributive configuration needs a precondition
it cannot produce: resource rents that are very large relative to the
citizen body. Rentier-state theory, from Beblawi and Luciani's distinction
between allocation and production states onward, links such rents to
states that secure consent through welfare rather than taxation (as
summarised in a 2021 review; see also Gray's theory of "late rentierism,"
Georgetown University in Qatar, 2011). But rents do not reliably produce
distribution. Oman and Equatorial Guinea had similar resource rents per
person, a little over US$8,000 in 1995–2014, yet illustrate opposite
outcomes, generosity and neglect (*American Political Science Review*,
2023, citing Ross). The configuration's successes are small, high-rent Gulf
monarchies: one regional and cultural context.

**Score Justification:** Structural failure (0.0) — the configuration works
only where a precondition it cannot create is present, and no credible
generalization argument exists (the second clause of the H.7 0.0 anchor).
This differs from Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism's 0.5, which scored a fund
mechanism found in Norway, Alaska, New Zealand, and Timor-Leste; the Gulf
configuration adds a distributive state and a migrant-labour regime that
those cases lack. **Flagged as contestable:** a reader applying that
precedent, or weighting institutional variation across the six Gulf
monarchies, would score 0.5. Consequence, checked: 9.5/26 with 9 failures;
tier unchanged.

**Domain 5 = 1.0 + 0.5 + 1.0 + 0.5 + 0.0 = 3.0/5 (60%)**

## Final Assessment

**Key Strengths:**

- **Domain 5 (3.0/5):** decades of operation at national scale, and
  designed coexistence with a market economy.
- **Deep buffers:** a large sovereign fund, central-bank reserves, fiscal
  discipline that the IMF credits, and output losses below 20% in three
  compound shocks (C3.3).
- **Generous allocation to citizens:** land, housing finance, public
  employment, and a contributory pension (C1.2a, C1.3). Counting citizens
  only raises the total by 4.0 points (Summary Scores).

**Key Deficiencies:**

- **Domain 4 (0.5/5) ties China and Status Quo Market Capitalism for the
  lowest in the corpus.** Four of its five criteria are structural
  failures, none of them flagged.
- **Ten failures.** Qatar has the lowest total (9.0/26) and the most
  failures (10) of the eight Step 1b systems.
- **The non-citizen majority** is the thread running through C1.1–C1.5,
  C2.1, C2.2, C2.5, C4.3, and C4.5, which is why the population-scope
  decision matters so much.

**Four comparative findings (every formal claim checked by
`verify_qatar.py`).**

1. **The mechanism and the configuration.** Sovereign Wealth Fund Statism,
   which scored the fund mechanism alone, stands at 14.0/26 with 3
   failures against 9.0/26 with 10: a difference of 5.0 points and 7
   failures. The two differ on 12 criteria, and Qatar is higher on only
   one, C1.3, because of its citizens' housing scheme. They share two
   failures, C4.2 and C4.5. The domain deltas (Qatar minus Sovereign Wealth
   Fund Statism) are +0.0, -2.0, -1.0, -1.5, -0.5. Embedding the fund in a
   rentier-distributive state with a disenfranchised migrant workforce
   costs most in Human Autonomy and Ethical Integrity.
2. **The siblings.** Singapore strictly dominates Qatar: it is higher on 10
   criteria and lower on none, and the domain deltas (Qatar minus
   Singapore) are -1.5, -1.0, -1.5, -0.5, -0.5. Singapore's 4 failures are
   a subset of Qatar's 10. Neither China nor Qatar dominates the other.
   China and Qatar differ on only 4 criteria: Qatar is higher on C5.3 and
   lower on C1.5, C3.2, and C5.5. China's 8 failures are a subset of
   Qatar's 10, and China's total is higher by 1.0 point.
3. **Corpus position.** In a prospective 21-system corpus, Qatar would rank
   20th of 21, above only Libertarian Minarchism (8.0/26), with no exact
